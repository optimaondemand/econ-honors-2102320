📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Who decides how many boots, tractors, and loaves of bread a huge country will make next year, and what happens when that person is wrong?
After Lenin, the Soviet Union tried something no large country had tried before: planning almost every part of the economy from the center. This lesson looks at how that plan worked, what it built, and what it cost. As always, the aim is to report the evidence fairly, including the parts that are still argued over.

📅 PART 1 · STALIN BUILDS THE PLAN
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Imagine you are the single planner for a whole country. You have limited steel, workers, and farmland. Which would you put first? (No wrong answer. You may change your mind.)

Lenin died in January 1924. A struggle for power followed, and by the late 1920s Joseph Stalin had pushed his rivals aside. The New Economic Policy was still in place, but trouble was building. In 1927 the state could not buy enough grain at the low prices it was offering, and the cities and the army needed food. Stalin's answer was to move toward force and away from the NEP. By the fall of 1928 he had decided to end it.

The replacement was a national plan. A state commission called Gosplan drew up targets for the whole economy, and its work became the basis of the first Five-Year Plan, which began in 1928. Gosplan's planners listed how much steel, coal, and machinery the country should produce. Then each factory and farm was told its share.

Was this turn inevitable? Historians say no. Nikolai Bukharin and other leaders argued for keeping the NEP and letting farmers and markets keep feeding the cities. Stalin and his allies won the argument, and in November 1929 Bukharin was removed from the top leadership. Another outcome was possible, one in which the Soviet Union industrialized more slowly and kept its markets. We cannot know how that road would have gone, but we can see that it existed.

A Soviet collective farm. It was farmed as a group, under state supervision, instead of by separate families.
Kulak /koo-LAHK/
A Russian word that means "fist." The Soviet government used it as a label for better-off farmers and for anyone it blamed for resisting.

You met this idea in Module 1. A command economy answers the three basic questions, what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom, through orders from the center. Under the Five-Year Plan, the answer to the first question was heavy industry: steel, coal, electric power, and machines. The reasoning was that an industrial base would make the country strong, fast. According to Britannica, the cost was a sharp fall in consumer goods, the things families actually buy.

📈 PART 2 · WHAT THE PLAN BUILT, AND HOW HARD IT IS TO KNOW

Here is a made-up example, to show how a target can go wrong in a small way. Suppose a boot factory is told to produce 10,000 pairs this year, and it is judged on that count. The easiest way to hit the target is to make one size, in one style, from the cheapest leather. The factory meets its quota. But the stores now have thousands of identical boots and no boots for people with narrow feet or large feet. The plan counted pairs. It did not count whether anyone could use them.

Try this in your notebook before you click: how could a planner change the target so the factory makes useful boots? Then ask what new information the planner would need.
The planner could set targets by size and style. But that requires knowing how many people in every town wear every size, and how tastes shift from year to year. In a market, shoe stores report what sells through their orders, and prices on slow sellers fall. A planner must collect that knowledge from the whole country, and that takes time. This is the problem you will study directly in Lesson 7.04.

The scale of the effort was real. The number of workers in industry roughly doubled during the first plan, from about 3.1 million in 1928 to about 6 million in 1932, according to the historian Phillip Hansen. New mines and factories went up. The plan's own heavy-industry target was a rise of 350 percent. Stalin announced in 1933 that the plan had been fulfilled in four years and three months.

Here is where a careful reader slows down. How much did output actually rise? Estimates differ widely. One historian, Keefe, puts the rise in overall industrial output at about 50 percent. Another, Dobb, reports a rise of 118 percent, with capital goods up 158 percent and consumer goods up 87 percent. The gap is not a typo. It comes from different ways of counting, and it shows a problem with planned economies that you should keep in mind. When the people who run the system also report on the system, the numbers can reflect what the system wants to hear. Historians describe 1930 as an era of exaggeration in the official claims.

On the plus side, many historians argue that the industrial base built in these years helped the Soviet Union survive the German invasion of 1941. That is a real first-order result, since defense is one of a regime's first jobs. On the minus side, the same plan left families short of basic goods. Britannica notes that consumer goods fell sharply, and Hansen and other historians report that food became scarce in the cities.

Notice a pattern. Planners could set a target for steel and get steel. What they could not easily do was find out what people wanted, or whether a factory was making the right thing. A manager rewarded for meeting a target will meet the target, even if that means making goods nobody needs. Targets were often raised after they were met, according to the historian Sheila Fitzpatrick. That teaches a manager to hold something back. You will meet this idea again in the final lesson.

🌾 PART 3 · COLLECTIVIZATION AND THE FAMINE

Industry needed workers, and cities needed bread. Stalin's government decided to get both from the countryside through collectivization. The plan was to gather millions of small family farms into large collective farms, called kolkhozy, run as a group under state supervision. The state would then buy the grain at low prices set by the state, feed the cities, and sell some abroad to pay for imported machines.

Farmers who owned more land or animals than their neighbors were called kulaks. Stalin called for an end to them as a class. The label was stretched to cover anyone who resisted, and one estimate counts about 3.5 million people as stripped of their property between 1929 and 1931. Some historians report that farmers slaughtered their own animals rather than hand them over. Think about that choice in terms of incentives. A farmer who cannot keep the reward for raising an animal may decide that eating it is the better option.

Then the harvests failed in places, and the plan did not bend. The numbers that follow come from a standard reference article and are cited there to specialist historians, so treat them as approximate. In 1930, Ukraine supplied about 38 percent of the state's grain deliveries while producing about 27 percent of the harvest. In 1931 its harvest fell from 23.9 million tons to 18.3 million tons, yet the demand stayed near the earlier year's level of 7.7 million tons.

Try this in your notebook before you click: if the harvest was 18.3 million tons and the state demanded 7.7 million tons, about what percent of the whole harvest was taken?
7.7 divided by 18.3 is about 0.42, so roughly 42 percent. Compare that with the farm family's own needs, seed for next year, and feed for animals. A demand that large leaves very little margin, and it shows how a quota set from the center can ignore what happened in the field.

Grain exports did fall sharply, but the damage was already done. The result was a famine in 1932 and 1933 that struck Ukraine, the Volga region, and Kazakhstan hardest. Estimates of the dead range from about 3 million to nearly 9 million, depending on the historian and the way deaths are counted. The government denied the famine and refused foreign aid. Peasants who took grain from the fields could be punished. Passports and travel limits kept people from leaving the hungry regions.

Kazakhstan deserves a mention of its own. Many Kazakhs lived by herding animals on open land, and the collectivization campaign hit that way of life hard. The historian Sarah Cameron reports that somewhere between 665,000 and 1.1 million Kazakhs fled the famine, and that about 30 percent of those refugees died of disease and hunger. A nomadic people lost much of its herd, and with it, much of its way of living.

Numbers like these can slide past us. Try holding one in mind. The low estimate of about 3 million deaths is equal to the crowd in a stadium of 100,000 seats filled thirty times over. Behind each seat is a person who needed bread. That is the weight you are asked to carry into the analysis, not push away from it.

What historians argue about

Was the famine a deliberate act or a catastrophe of policy and rigidity? Scholars disagree, and a fair student reports both sides. J. Arch Getty, R. W. Davies, and Stephen Wheatcroft argue that it came from "bungling and rigidity," as Getty puts it, and not from a plan to kill. Michael Ellman argues that knowingly keeping policies in place that cause mass death can be a form of intent. A third group, writing on Ukraine, argues that the famine fell harder on Ukrainians than policy alone explains. All of them agree on the central fact: a government's economic plan, enforced without regard to what it was doing to farmers, was followed by millions of deaths.

💡 WHEN A SYSTEM CANNOT HEAR BAD NEWS
A market has a built-in way to hear bad news: prices rise, profits fall, and firms change what they do. A command economy depends on reports flowing up and orders flowing down. When reporting a failure is dangerous, the center loses its eyes. That is one reason a mistake in a command economy can grow so large before anyone fixes it.

Judging the plan by first-order problems

Use the same test as before. On defense and industrial power, the plan can claim results. On the first-order economic problem, producing and distributing enough that the whole population prospers, the record is poor. The country had less food and fewer consumer goods than its people needed, and millions died in a famine. Virtues and vices here are stable traits, not single deeds. A leadership that is determined, but also secretive, rigid, and willing to deny a famine, shows traits that make a regime worse at learning from its mistakes.
