📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What happens when a theory written for factory towns meets a country of farmers at war?
Marx expected communism to arrive first in the most industrial nations. Instead it arrived in Russia, a huge farming country in the middle of a world war. This lesson follows what Lenin and his party did with the theory, why their first economic plan nearly wrecked the country, and why Lenin himself turned back.

📚 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth a look before you read. Each one is bold and underlined in the text. Hover over it for a quick definition, or tap the speaker to hear it said.
Communism /KOM-yuh-niz-um/
The idea that the main tools of production should belong to everyone together instead of to private owners. It is also the name of the political movement built on that idea.
Famine /FAM-in/
A severe shortage of food that leaves many people hungry and causes many deaths.
Manifesto /man-ih-FES-toh/
A public statement that explains the goals and beliefs of a person or a group.
Nationalize /NASH-uh-nuh-lyz/
To take over private businesses or property and make them owned by the government.
New Economic Policy (NEP) /noo ek-uh-NOM-ik POL-ih-see/
The Soviet policy that began in 1921. It ended forced grain seizures and allowed small private trade and farming for profit.
Provisional Government /pruh-VIZH-uh-nul GUV-ern-ment/
A temporary government set up to rule until a lasting one can be chosen.
Requisition /rek-wih-ZISH-un/
To demand and take goods, such as grain, for the government's use, often with little or no payment.
🇷🇺 PART 1 · A COUNTRY AT BREAKING POINT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
You are a farmer in 1918. A government agent arrives and says the state will take your surplus grain at a price the state has set, and that price is low. What would you plant next year? (No wrong answer. You may change your mind.)

Before the war story, a puzzle. Marx wrote for an industrial world, and Russia was not one. It had some large factories in cities such as Petrograd and Moscow, but most Russians were farmers. A strict reading of the Manifesto would have told Lenin to wait. He chose not to. He argued that a small, disciplined party could lead the city workers and win the farmers' support, and he gave the farmers a promise in three words: "Peace, land, and bread." Whether that was a bold adaptation of the theory or a break from it is a question historians still debate.

Russia entered World War I in 1914. By 1917 the war had drained the country. Food was short in the cities, millions of soldiers had died or been wounded, and many people had lost faith in the ruler, Tsar Nicholas II. In March 1917, by the calendar the Russians used then it was February, the tsar gave up his throne. A provisional government took charge. It promised elections and a new constitution, and it kept Russia in the war.

That last choice mattered. Soldiers and city workers wanted peace and bread. The provisional government was weak and divided, and it could give them neither. Into that gap stepped a small, disciplined party led by Vladimir Lenin: the Bolsheviks. In November 1917, again by our calendar, they took control of the capital, Petrograd, and declared themselves the new government.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Two Russian words you will meet in this lesson. The first names Lenin's party. The second names its secret police. Italic type and a dotted underline mark each one in the text.
Bolshevik /BOHL-shuh-vik/
A member of the Russian party led by Lenin. The name comes from a Russian word meaning "majority."
Cheka /CHEK-uh/
The secret police the Bolsheviks created in December 1917. The name is a short form of a longer Russian title.

Pause here, because historians insist on one point. None of this was certain. The Bolsheviks were not a large party. Had the provisional government made peace, or acted on the farmers' hunger for land, or held its election on better terms, the story could have gone another way. Lenin saw an opening and moved fast. That is decisiveness, and in a crisis it can decide a nation's fate. It is also a trait that cuts both ways, since it can serve a good cause or a bad one.

Once in power, the Bolsheviks did not leave their rule to chance. In December 1917 they created a secret police, the Cheka. In January 1918 their forces broke up the elected assembly after a single meeting. They made peace with Germany in March 1918. Then a civil war began between the Bolsheviks, called the Reds, and a patchwork of opponents, called the Whites. It raged for about three years. A government that has to feed its army, its cities, and its allies, with its country in ruins, has a first-order economic problem as well as a political one.

⚙️ PART 2 · WAR COMMUNISM, 1918 TO 1921

Lenin's government now tried to run a country's economy by decree. Historians call the policy War Communism, and it had three main parts.

PolicyWhat it didDate given
Grain requisitionArmed squads were sent to take grain from farm families. Central planners set the quotas.A decree in May 1918 gave the food agency power to seize grain by force
Nationalization of industryThe state took over large factories, then mines, and later small workshops.June 1918 for large industry; November 1920 for small workshops
Ban on private tradeBuying and selling for profit was outlawed, and markets were closed.November 1918

Each of these made sense to its authors. If the economy was a war machine, the army and the cities had to be fed, and the state would take what it needed. If capitalists were the enemy, nationalizing their factories removed them. And if profit was the sickness, then ending trade ended the sickness. Notice that the policy fit the Manifesto's goals, since the state took ownership of the means of production and put planning in the place of markets.

But think about the farmer's choices again. A farmer who knows that any surplus will be taken has no reason to grow one. So the policy created the very shortage it was meant to solve. This is the chain you studied in Module 2, when price controls and fixed prices pushed producers to supply less.

Try this in your notebook before you click: the harvest fell from 80.1 million tons to 46.5 million tons. By how many tons did it fall, and what percent of the 1913 harvest is that?
80.1 minus 46.5 is 33.6 million tons. Divide 33.6 by 80.1 and you get about 0.42, so the harvest fell by roughly 42 percent. Notice that this is a fall in what farmers produced, not a change in how many people needed to eat.

Those harvest figures come from the historian David Christian, and they tell a plain story. Of course, the war and the civil war also wrecked fields, railroads, and farms, so the policy was not the only cause. Honest historians weigh the causes differently. But a famine that followed in 1921 and 1922 is usually estimated to have killed about five million people, and the range of estimates runs wider than that. War, drought along the Volga River, and the policy itself all played a part.

📜 PRIMARY SOURCE
"Our too hasty, rigid, unprepared 'communism' ... was necessitated by the war and the impossibility of either obtaining commodities or setting the factories going."
V. I. Lenin, notes for a speech at the Tenth Congress of the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks), March 1921, on replacing grain requisition with a tax. Marxists Internet Archive, Lenin Collected Works. Ellipsis marks words left out.

Read that quotation slowly. Lenin himself, in March 1921, called the policy "too hasty, rigid, unprepared." He also defends it as something the war required. Both parts matter. A leader who can say his own policy was too rigid has a useful quality, the willingness to look at results. A leader who blames the war for everything has an excuse that historians should test, not accept.

🔄 PART 3 · THE RETREAT: THE NEW ECONOMIC POLICY

By early 1921, the cost of the policy was impossible to ignore. Peasants in some provinces rose up against the grain squads. In early March, sailors at the Kronstadt naval base near Petrograd rebelled too. These sailors had once been strong supporters of the Bolsheviks. Accounts agree that they were unhappy with the food policy, and many also say they demanded free elections. The Red Army crushed the rising, and thousands were killed.

Within days, at the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921, Lenin changed course. The grain squads would stop. In their place, farmers would pay a fixed tax, paid in grain, and after paying it they could sell what was left on the open market. That is the New Economic Policy, or NEP. Small shops and trade came back. The state kept its hold on the largest industries and on the banks, but the bargain with the farmer had changed.

Lenin knew exactly what this meant. In his own working notes he wrote down the question, "Back to capitalism?" He also described freedom of turnover for peasants as "freedom to trade," which in his words meant "freedom of capitalism." He called it a "breathing space." He did not claim it was a victory for the theory. He was admitting that the theory, run at full strength, had been too much for the country to bear.

💡 INCENTIVES ARE NOT A TRICK
When Lenin let farmers keep the surplus, farm output began to recover. You do not have to like markets to see what happened: people work harder when they keep some of the reward. The economist's idea of an incentive is simply the observation that people respond to what a choice costs and what it earns them. A government can ignore that idea, but it cannot make the idea go away.

Behind the numbers are people. A famine that kills about five million people is not a statistic to move past quickly. Each of those deaths was a person with a name, a family, and a village. Optima asks you to hold the arithmetic and the human weight together. The numbers tell you how big the failure was. They do not tell you what it felt like to live inside it.

How to judge Lenin: first-order problems

Optima asks you to judge a regime by whether it solves the first-order problems of any society. The first political problems are peace and order at home and defense against enemies. The first economic problem is producing and distributing enough that the whole population prospers.

By that standard, the record is mixed, and it should be reported that way. On defense, the Bolsheviks won the civil war, and that took organization and will. On order, they built a state that lasted, but they did it with a secret police and with force against their own sailors and farmers. On the economy, the first plan failed badly, and Lenin had the clear-sightedness to turn back. The traits that appear in this story are decisiveness, adaptability, and a hard ruthlessness toward opponents. A fair account names all three.

One more point about the shape of history. The NEP lasted from 1921 until 1928, and nobody at the time knew whether it was a permanent change or a pause. Lenin died in January 1924. Which way the country went after that, whether toward markets or back toward command, turned partly on who won a struggle for power. In the next lesson you will see how Joseph Stalin settled that question, and what it cost.
