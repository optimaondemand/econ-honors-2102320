📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can any group of experts, however smart, gather what millions of people know about their own needs and circumstances?
You have now followed an idea from a London pamphlet to a Soviet plan. This final lesson of Module 7 asks the question underneath it all: why did central planning run into trouble again and again? You will learn one of the best-known answers in economics, test it against the evidence, and look at how the Soviet Union ended.

💡 PART 1 · THE KNOWLEDGE PROBLEM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A school cafeteria has to decide how many slices of pizza to make for Friday. Who is best placed to know how many slices will be eaten? (No wrong answer. You may change your mind.)

Here is a puzzle that is easy to say and hard to solve. An economy contains millions of people who each know something that nobody else knows. A shop owner knows that a new family moved into the apartment above her store. A farmer knows that his north field floods in spring. A truck driver knows that a bridge is closed. None of this appears in any official report. Yet an economy works well only if decisions are made with that kind of knowledge.

In 1945, the economist F. A. Hayek published an essay called "The Use of Knowledge in Society." Its argument is the heart of what economists call the knowledge problem. Hayek said the economic problem of society is not simply how to use resources that everyone knows about. It is how to make use of knowledge that is spread across millions of minds and that no one person or committee will ever hold in full. An earlier economist, Ludwig von Mises, had made a related argument in 1920: without real market prices for tools and materials, planners cannot calculate which plans are sensible. Hayek shared the 1974 Nobel Memorial Prize in economics.

A note on honesty: this lesson explains Hayek's argument in plain words and quotes none of his text. If you read the essay yourself, judge for yourself whether this summary is fair.

Prices as messages

Hayek's answer to the puzzle is the price system. A price is a message, and it carries information to everyone who needs it without telling them why. He used the example of tin. Suppose that somewhere in the world a new use for tin is found, or one of the sources of tin dries up. A buyer of tin does not need to know which one happened. All the buyer sees is that the price has risen. That is enough. The buyer uses less tin or switches to a substitute, and so do millions of others. Together, they adjust, and no commission had to give an order.

Something happens far away: a tin source is lost.
➡
Sellers see a shortage and raise the price.
➡
Buyers everywhere see the higher price.
➡
Each buyer, knowing only that price, uses less tin or switches materials.
➡
The shortage is eased without anyone giving an order.

You have seen pieces of this idea already. In Module 2, you studied how prices rise when something is scarce and fall when it is plentiful. In Lesson 4.04, you saw what happens when the price signal is blocked, as in gasoline lines under a price cap. Hayek takes the idea one step further. The price is not just a number. It is a way of passing knowledge from people who have it to people who need it.

Now return to the Soviet planners. They had to set the prices, set the targets, and issue the orders, and they had to do it for a country of many millions of people and a huge variety of goods. They could collect statistics. What they could not easily collect was the local, changing knowledge that Hayek describes. That knowledge is in the heads of the shop owner, the farmer, and the truck driver. By the time a report climbed up to Moscow and an order came back down, the situation had changed.

💡 A FAIR QUESTION: WHAT ABOUT COMPUTERS?
In 1945 there were no computers like ours. Today, companies and governments can collect and process far more data than any planner of the 1930s could dream of. Economists still disagree about how much that changes the problem. Some argue that the knowledge problem is now smaller. Others argue that the most useful knowledge is still local, changing, and often unspoken, and that no database holds it. Be ready to defend whichever side you find stronger.

💰 PART 2 · INCENTIVES: WHO GAINS WHEN THINGS GO WELL?

Knowledge is one problem. Motivation is another. People respond to what a choice costs and what it earns them, which economists call incentives. In a market economy, a worker's earnings tend to follow what the worker contributes, and a firm that serves customers well makes a profit while a firm that does not takes a loss. You studied this in Module 2, in the work on workers' earnings. Pay signals where workers are most needed, and workers respond.

In a command economy, those links are cut or weakened. The state sets wages, the state sets prices, and the state sets the targets a manager must reach. A manager's reward comes from meeting the plan, not from pleasing buyers. So the manager's best strategy is to make sure the plan is easy to meet. Ask for generous supplies, promise less than you can do, and avoid risks. You have already seen what happens when a target is judged on the wrong measure, as with the boots in Lesson 7.03. A worker also has little reason to work harder when the paycheck stays the same.

Where we saw incentives at workWhat the incentive wasWhat people did

This pattern is why economists treat incentives as a first principle. You do not have to assume people are selfish. You only have to notice that when effort goes unrewarded and failure goes unpunished, over time less effort is made and fewer risks are taken. The market has its own flaws, and you have studied several in this course, including externalities and unequal outcomes. But the market has a built-in way to reward good guesses and to punish bad ones, and the command economy had a much harder time doing either.

🏁 PART 3 · HOW THE SOVIET UNION ENDED

By the 1980s the Soviet Union had a huge army, a vast territory, and an economy that had stopped keeping pace with the West. Mikhail Gorbachev became the Communist Party's leader in 1985. He did not set out to end the Soviet Union. He wanted to repair it. He launched two reforms, perestroika, meaning restructuring, and glasnost, meaning openness. The first loosened some of the central controls on the economy. The second allowed more public criticism than the country had known in decades.

Reform and openness did not rescue the system. Instead they let long-hidden problems and long-held grievances come out in the open. In the republics that made up the union, from the Baltic states to Ukraine, calls for independence grew. The end came quickly. On December 8, 1991, the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus signed the Belovezha Accords, declaring that the Soviet Union was ending. On December 25, Gorbachev resigned. On December 26, the Soviet parliament formally voted the Union out of existence, and the red flag came down from the Kremlin. We call that last event the dissolution of the Soviet Union.

Why did it happen? Be careful here, because historians do not agree on one cause. Most point to several at once: an economy that grew slowly and wasted resources, the cost of the arms race and a long war in Afghanistan, falling oil prices in the 1980s, and strong feelings of national identity in the republics. Others stress the role of particular decisions, since a different leader or a different set of reforms might have produced a different result. A good student of history says what the evidence supports and resists a single tidy explanation. The economic problems you have studied in this module were part of the story, but they were not the whole of it.

🇰🇷 PART 4 · PROPERTY RIGHTS AND PROSPERITY: THE TWO KOREAS

In Lesson 5.04 you read about the Korean peninsula as a kind of natural experiment. After World War II, the peninsula was divided into two countries. The people had the same language, history, and culture. The two governments chose very different systems. North Korea built a command economy under a single ruling party. South Korea built an economy based on private property, contracts, and trade. Today, we can measure what happened.

$41,321
South Korea, GDP per person, 2022
$1,569
North Korea, GDP per person, 2022
about 26 to 1
the ratio between the two figures

Those numbers come from the Maddison Project Database (2023 edition), posted by Our World in Data. They are measured in 2011 international dollars, a unit that adjusts for what money buys in each place. Per person means the country's total output divided by its population. By this measure, the average person in South Korea has about 26 times the output of the average person in North Korea.

Try this in your notebook before you click: divide 41,321 by 1,569. What do you get, and what does it mean?
41,321 divided by 1,569 is about 26.3. It means that the output per person in South Korea is roughly 26 times larger. Notice that this compares averages, not every person. It also tells you nothing by itself about why the gap exists.

Be careful with that last point. A responsible economist adds three cautions. First, North Korea's figure is an estimate, because the government releases little reliable data. Second, the two countries differ in many ways beyond property rights. They fought a destructive war, received different foreign help, and had different political systems. Historians also note that much of the peninsula's heavy industry in 1945 sat in the north, which makes the gap more striking, not less. Third, one comparison cannot prove a rule.

But the comparison fits a pattern you have now seen in many places. Where people can own what they produce, sign contracts that will be enforced, and keep the reward of their effort, they tend to work, invest, and take risks. Where those protections are weak, they tend not to. The Korean gap, the Soviet harvests, and the NEP rebound all point the same way. That is the benchmark your standards ask you to understand: the link between secure property rights and prosperity.

A balanced verdict

Let us finish the module with the test Optima uses. Did the command economy solve the first-order problems? On defense and heavy industry, it showed real results for a time. On producing and distributing enough that the whole population prospers, the evidence is poor, and the price in human lives in the 1930s was enormous. That is not the same as saying markets are perfect. They produce unequal outcomes and costs that fall on third parties, and Marx's question about whether working people share in a society's wealth is still worth asking. The honest conclusion is narrower. Whatever its goals, a system that cannot gather knowledge, reward effort, or hear bad news will struggle to feed its people.

The next module changes direction. You have spent two weeks studying how whole economies are organized. In Module 8, you will turn to a question that is personal, and urgent for any adult: what does it cost to borrow money, and how do you make a wise decision about it?
