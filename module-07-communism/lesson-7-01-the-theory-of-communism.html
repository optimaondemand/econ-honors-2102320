📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If one class owns the factories and another class works in them, is that a fair arrangement, and what would you have to change to fix it?
In 1848, two young men in Europe published a short pamphlet that went on to shape the lives of hundreds of millions of people. This lesson reads what they actually wrote, in their own words, before anyone else interpreted it. Your job is to understand the argument well enough to state it fairly, and then to test it the way an economist would.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state the main argument of The Communist Manifesto in your own words, using the terms class struggle, bourgeoisie, and proletariat.
2
I will be able to explain what Marx and Engels meant by "abolition of private property" and describe the ten measures they proposed.
3
I will be able to evaluate the theory fairly by asking what it explains well and what it leaves unanswered about how an economy would actually run.
📚 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth a look before you read. Each one is bold and underlined in the text. Hover over it for a quick definition, or tap the speaker to hear it said.
Bourgeoisie /boor-zhwah-ZEE/
In Marx's writing, the class of people who own factories, land, and other capital and who hire others to work for them.
Class Struggle /klas STRUG-ul/
In Marx's view, the long conflict between groups, such as owners and workers, over who controls production and who gets its rewards.
Communism /KOM-yuh-niz-um/
The idea that the main tools of production should belong to everyone together instead of to private owners. It is also the name of the political movement built on that idea.
Manifesto /man-ih-FES-toh/
A public statement that explains the goals and beliefs of a person or a group.
Proletariat /proh-leh-TAIR-ee-ut/
In Marx's writing, the class of workers who own no factories or land and who live by selling their labor for wages.
📜 PART 1 · TWO MEN AND A PAMPHLET
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read, think about a simple question. Why do some families end up owning the factory, the farm, or the shop, while others work for a paycheck? (No wrong answer. You may change your mind.)

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels were two German thinkers who became friends in the 1840s. Marx was a journalist and philosopher who had been forced to move from country to country because of his political writing. Engels came from a family that owned a cotton mill, so he had seen factory life up close from the owner's side. Together they wrote a pamphlet for a small group called the Communist League. It was published in London in 1848 and is known today as The Communist Manifesto.

A manifesto is a public statement of aims, and this one does not start timidly. It begins by telling the reader that the thing people call "communism" has become a European worry, and that the authors mean to answer the rumors by stating the party's views openly. It is short, vivid, and written to persuade. Keep that in mind. This is a work of argument, and it was meant to move readers, not just inform them.

💡 WHY SLOW DOWN FOR AN IDEA YOU MAY END UP REJECTING?
You will meet many opinions about Marx in your life, from people who love him and people who hate him. Very few of them have read the pamphlet. Honest scholars state a view so well that its own supporters would say, "Yes, that is what we mean." Only then do they test it.
⚖️ PART 2 · THE ARGUMENT: HISTORY AS CLASS STRUGGLE

Marx and Engels open the first chapter with a sweeping claim about the past. In their English translation, it reads: "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles." Hitherto means "up to now." They are saying that if you look at any society in recorded history, you will find groups pulling against each other. They list pairs that you already know from your history classes: "Freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf, guild-master and journeyman, in a word, oppressor and oppressed."

That is the core idea of class struggle. In every age, they argue, one group controls the main ways of making a living, and another group works under that control. The ancient world had masters and slaves. The medieval world had lords and serfs. They believed their own age had simplified things further. Here is how they put it: "Society as a whole is more and more splitting up into two great hostile camps, into two great classes directly facing each other." They named the two classes the bourgeoisie and the proletariat.

ClassWho they are, in the Manifesto's termsHow they make a living
BourgeoisieThe owners of factories, land, ships, banks, and other capitalThey own the means of production, meaning the tools, buildings, and land used to make goods, and they hire workers
ProletariatThe wage workers who own no capitalThey sell their labor for wages, because they have nothing else to sell

Notice that this is a claim about how people earn their living, not about how rich they are. A wealthy doctor who owns no factory would not fit neatly into either box. The theory sorts people by their relationship to capital.

What the authors admired, and what they charged

Here is a surprise for many students. Marx and Engels did not simply hate the owners. They wrote with real admiration about what the bourgeoisie had done. Modern industry, they say, had "established the world market," and they describe a class that built cities, ships, railroads, and factories faster than any ruling group before it. They saw capitalism as a force of astonishing productive power.

Their complaint was about what that power did to people. They charged that the bourgeoisie had "resolved personal worth into exchange value," meaning that everything, including a worker's time and effort, had been reduced to a price. And they argued that the workers who ran the machines did not share in what the machines produced. The owner kept the profit. The worker kept a wage that, in their view, was just enough to live on.

Economists have argued over that last claim for more than a century. For now, just see the shape of the argument. If one side holds the tools and the other side holds nothing, Marx and Engels thought, the struggle must end in revolution.

📜 PRIMARY SOURCE
"The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles."

"Society as a whole is more and more splitting up into two great hostile camps, into two great classes directly facing each other."
Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, Manifesto of the Communist Party (1848), Chapter 1, English translation by Samuel Moore, 1888, edited by Frederick Engels. Text from the Marxists Internet Archive.

Read those two lines once for what they claim and once for how they sound. The language is bold and total. "All hitherto existing society" is a very large claim, and a careful reader asks what evidence could support it. Wars between nations, religious divisions, and family rivalries do not sort neatly into two classes. You might say the authors found a real pattern and then stretched it to cover everything. That is a fair thing to say, and a fair thing to test.

📜 PART 3 · ABOLISH PRIVATE PROPERTY? WHAT THEY PROPOSED

Chapter 2 turns from diagnosis to remedy, and it contains the sentence that everyone remembers. "In this sense," the authors write, "the theory of the Communists may be summed up in the single sentence: Abolition of private property." If you stop reading there, it sounds as if they want to take away your house, your phone, and your savings. Read further and the target becomes narrower. Their aim, as they explain it, was the ownership of capital: the factories, the land, and the other productive assets that let one class hire another. The theory called for those assets to be owned in common instead.

That, in short, is communism as the Manifesto describes it. The tools of production belong to everyone through the community, and so no one can build a fortune by hiring others to work them. The authors said the working class would first need to win power. In their words, "The proletariat will use its political supremacy to wrest, by degrees, all capital from the bourgeoisie." The phrase "by degrees" matters. They did not expect it to happen in a day.

The ten measures

At the end of Chapter 2, the authors list ten steps that they said would be "pretty generally applicable" in the most advanced countries. This table summarizes them in plain words. It is a paraphrase, not a quotation, so check the full list in the original when you do the assignment.

#What they proposed (summary)What kind of change it is
1End private ownership of land and use land rents for public purposesOwnership of capital
2A steeply progressive income taxTaxation
3End the right of inheritanceOwnership of capital
4Take the property of emigrants and rebelsOwnership of capital
5Put credit in the hands of the state, through a national bankMoney and banking
6Put communication and transport under state controlOwnership of capital
7Expand state-owned factories and improve land by a common planPlanning
8Require everyone to work; form labor armies, especially for farmingPlanning
9Combine farming with manufacturing and gradually erase the line between town and countryPlanning
10Free public education for all children, and combine schooling with industrial productionEducation

Look at the list the way an economist would. Some items change who owns capital. Some change money and banking. Some are plans for what to build and where. A few look familiar: the United States today has a progressive income tax and free public schools. What set the Manifesto apart was the combination, and the goal behind it: to move almost all productive property into the hands of the state.

Try this in your notebook before you click: pick any three of the ten measures and decide whether each one changes who OWNS capital, how much people PAY in taxes, or what a central authority PLANS. Then compare with the note below.
There is no single right sorting, since a few measures do more than one thing. The pattern worth noticing is how many items give a central authority a decision that markets normally make: where credit goes, what is built, who works where. That pattern is the reason this lesson ends with a question about knowledge, which you will return to in Lesson 7.04.
🔍 PART 4 · TESTING THE THEORY

A theory is not true because it is powerful or because it inspires people. It is true if it explains what happens in the world. So put Marx and Engels through the same test you would give any claim in this course. A great and difficult text is a living argument, and you are entitled to answer it.

What the theory gets right

It sees something real. The early factory age produced both enormous wealth and harsh conditions for many workers, and the authors wrote while it was happening. They saw that industry would change politics, family life, and culture. And the question they pose, whether a society's wealth reaches its working people, is a serious one. Reformers who never accepted communism worked on it with child labor laws, wage laws, and safety rules.

What it leaves unanswered

Start with the prediction. Marx and Engels expected revolution to arrive first in the most industrial countries. That is not what happened. In much of the industrial world, wages and living standards rose over the next century, and the first communist revolutions came in Russia and China, which were mostly farming countries at the time. A failed prediction does not erase a theory, but it deserves to be noticed.

Now the deeper problem, and it is an economic one. Suppose the revolution succeeded and the community owned all productive capital. Someone would still have to decide how many shoes to make, where to build the next steel mill, and how much wheat to plant. In a market, prices and profits guide those decisions, and millions of people take part. The Manifesto's remedy leans on a central authority and on planning, but it says very little about how that authority would know what to do. In Module 1 you met the three questions every society must answer: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom.

Notice too that history does not follow a script. Marx wrote as if each stage of history led surely to the next. Events actually turn on choices, accidents, and the characters of particular people. Nothing about the Russian Revolution, as you will see next lesson, was guaranteed by a law of history. Real people made real decisions, and the outcome could have gone another way.

💡 ONE QUESTION TO CARRY INTO THE NEXT LESSON
A theory written in London in 1848 was put into practice in Russia in 1917, in a country that Marx never studied closely. In the next three lessons, you will follow what happened. Keep one question in mind: when the theory met a real economy, did it solve the first-order economic problem, which is producing and distributing enough that the whole population prospers?
