Six Characteristics of Good Money

Money needs durability (it must survive years of handling without corrosion or damage — U.S. bills use a cotton-linen blend; some countries use tear-resistant polymer notes), portability (the giant Rai stones of Yap could show wealth but could not be moved to pay for anything; today's banknotes, cards, and mobile payments solve this completely), divisibility (breaking into small denominations, down to fractional shares or cryptocurrency "satoshis"), uniformity (every $10 bill must be worth exactly the same, protected by watermarks and security features against counterfeit), a limited supply (unlimited money collapses in value — Zimbabwe and Venezuela both suffered hyperinflation when this broke down), and acceptability (people must actually trust and use it; the U.S. dollar functions as a global reserve currency precisely because of this trust).

Commodity Money vs. Fiat Money

Commodity money (gold, silver, cattle, salt) has value because the material itself is worth something, independent of its use as currency — but it is heavy, and its supply can fluctuate unpredictably with mining or harvests. Fiat money (a modern dollar bill) has no intrinsic value; it works purely because a government declares it legal tender and people trust the system behind it. Fiat money is far more practical to manage, but that trust has to be actively protected — which is exactly what central banks exist to do.

Four Functions of Money

Money works as a medium of exchange (replacing barter and its "double coincidence of wants" problem), a unit of account (a common measuring stick so a $50 pair of shoes and $2-per-pound apples are instantly comparable), a store of value (letting you save today's purchasing power for tomorrow, though inflation erodes this over time), and a standard of deferred payment (making mortgages, credit cards, and student loans possible by letting people promise future money for something today).

📚 KEY VOCABULARY

Durability — the ability of money to withstand physical use without wearing out.
Corrosion — the gradual destruction of a material through chemical reactions with the environment.
Counterfeit — a fake version of money or goods made to deceive people.
Portability — the ease with which money can be carried and transferred.
Rai Stones — large stone disks once used as currency on the island of Yap.
Banknote — paper currency issued by a government or central bank.
Divisibility — the ability of money to be broken into smaller units.
Denomination — the face value printed on a coin or bill.
Uniformity — the consistency that gives each unit of the same money equal value.
Watermark — a hidden design embedded in currency to prevent forgery.
Limited Supply — the restriction on how much money is available, to maintain its value.
Hyperinflation — an extreme rise in prices that rapidly destroys purchasing power.
Acceptability — general agreement among people to use something as valid payment.
Global Reserve Currency — a widely trusted currency held by central banks for international trade.
Commodity Money — currency with value because it's made of a materially valuable substance.
Fluctuate — to change or vary in level or value over time.
Fiat Money — government-declared money valued through trust, not intrinsic worth.
Intrinsic — belonging naturally to something, an essential quality.
Legal Tender — officially recognized money that must be accepted for debts.
Medium of Exchange — money's role in facilitating trade by replacing barter.
Barter — the direct exchange of goods and services without money.
Multinational Corporation — a company operating in multiple countries.
Unit of Account — a standard measure of value allowing prices to be compared.
Commodity — a raw material or primary good tradeable on the market.
Store of Value — money's ability to hold worth over time so it can be saved.
Purchasing Power — the amount of goods and services a given amount of money can buy.
Standard of Deferred Payment — money's function allowing borrowing and future repayment.
Credit — borrowing money now with a promise of repayment later.
Indispensable — absolutely necessary or essential.
