The Fed sets policy. Who actually hands you a loan?
The Federal Reserve sets the rules of the game, but it doesn't open your checking account or approve your mortgage. That work happens at the network of banks and financial institutions that translate Fed policy into services you actually use.

📚 PART 1 · THE INSTITUTIONS BETWEEN YOU AND YOUR MONEY

Three Kinds of Financial Institutions

Commercial banks (Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo) offer the full range of consumer and business services: checking, savings, loans, mortgages, credit cards. Credit unions are nonprofit cooperatives owned by their own members, which lets them return profits as lower fees and better rates in exchange for a smaller footprint. Investment banks (Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley) don't serve everyday consumers at all; they help corporations and governments raise capital through underwriting new securities and advise on mergers and acquisitions. Insurance companies, mutual funds, and pension funds round out the system, channeling savings into productive investment and managing risk at scale.

Credit: Borrowing Against the Future

Personal loans give a fixed sum repaid over time at a fixed or variable interest rate. Mortgages spread home purchases over decades; agencies like Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac buy mortgages from lenders and securitize them, which keeps housing credit flowing. Revolving credit (credit cards) lets you borrow, repay, and borrow again up to a limit. Businesses use lines of credit and commercial loans the same way individuals use personal loans, to smooth cash-flow fluctuations or fund expansion — this constant borrowing and repaying is what underpins a large share of everyday economic activity.

Saving and Investing: Building Wealth Over Time

Savings accounts are safe and liquid but earn modest interest; Certificates of Deposit (CDs) trade liquidity for a slightly higher rate by locking funds up for a set term. Brokerage accounts, 401(k)s, and IRAs open access to stocks, bonds, and exchange-traded funds (ETFs). Every investment has a risk-return profile: stocks in equity markets offer higher long-run returns but real short-term volatility (a nearly 50 percent drop during the 2008 crisis, before recovering); government bonds are far steadier but pay less.

Diversification: Not Putting All Your Eggs in One Basket

Diversification spreads investment across many assets, sectors, and regions so a loss in one area can be offset by gains elsewhere. A S&P 500 index fund automatically diversifies across 500 large companies, smoothing out the risk of any single stock's bad year. Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) extend diversification into real estate without buying property directly. Fintech platforms have democratized access to all of this — investing that once required a broker now happens through an app.

📚 KEY VOCABULARY

Credit Union — a member-owned financial cooperative, often with lower fees and better rates than commercial banks.
Nonprofit — an organization operating for purposes other than profit, reinvesting earnings into its mission.
Investment Bank — a financial institution that helps companies and governments raise capital and advises on mergers.
Underwriting — assessing and assuming the risk of issuing new securities on behalf of a company.
Mergers and Acquisitions — combining companies, or one company purchasing another, for growth or strategic goals.
Mutual Funds — investment vehicles pooling money from many investors into a diversified portfolio.
Pension Funds — funds that manage retirement savings on behalf of employees.
Portfolio — a collection of financial assets held by an individual or institution.
Fixed / Variable Interest Rate — a rate that stays constant, or one that changes with market conditions.
Deplete — to reduce or use up financial resources or funds.
Securitizing — pooling loans or assets and selling them as securities to investors.
Revolving Credit — credit that allows borrowing, repaying, and re-borrowing up to a set limit.
Fluctuation — a variation in the value, price, or rate of a financial asset over time.
Underpin — to provide support or a foundation for a financial system or structure.
Certificates of Deposit (CDs) — time-deposit accounts paying a fixed rate in exchange for leaving funds untouched.
Brokerage Accounts — accounts allowing investors to buy, sell, and hold securities.
401(k) — an employer-sponsored retirement savings plan using pre-tax contributions.
Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs) — personal retirement accounts with tax advantages.
Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs) — funds traded on exchanges holding a diversified collection of assets.
Risk-Return Profile — the relationship between an investment's potential risk and expected return.
Equity Markets — markets where shares of company ownership (stocks) are issued and traded.
S&P 500 — a stock index tracking 500 of the largest publicly traded U.S. companies.
Diversification — spreading investments across different assets to reduce overall risk.
Mitigate — to reduce, manage, or offset financial risk or loss.
Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) — companies owning or financing income-producing real estate, sold in shares.
Democratize — to make financial services or investments accessible to a broader population.
