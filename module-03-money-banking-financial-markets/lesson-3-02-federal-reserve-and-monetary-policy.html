📚 PART 1 · THE FED'S JOB AND ITS TOOLS

Why the Fed Exists

Congress created the Federal Reserve in 1913 after decades of financial panics, including the severe panic of 1907, when banks collapsed and credit froze. The Fed does not serve individual customers the way a commercial bank does; instead it supervises the banking system and acts as the "lender of last resort" — stepping in with emergency loans when banks cannot find funding elsewhere, as it did during the 2008 financial crisis. Its structure blends public and private, national and regional: a seven-member Board of Governors in Washington (14-year terms, designed to insulate them from short-term politics) plus twelve regional Reserve Banks that process payments and supervise local banks. The Fed doesn't need Congress's approval for its interest-rate decisions, but its Chair testifies before Congress and its meeting minutes are published — independence balanced against transparency.

Monetary Policy: The Fed's Steering Wheel

Monetary policy is how the Fed manages the money supply and interest rates to pursue maximum employment and stable prices at once. Its main tools: the federal funds rate (the rate banks charge each other overnight, which ripples out to every other interest rate in the economy), open market operations (buying or selling government securities to add or remove money from circulation — the Fed bought trillions in bonds during COVID-19, called "quantitative easing"), and reserve requirements (the share of deposits banks were once required to hold back rather than lend; the Fed reduced this requirement to zero percent in March 2020, so it no longer limits bank lending).

Expansionary vs. Contractionary Policy

Expansionary monetary policy lowers rates and injects liquidity to fight recession and high unemployment. Contractionary monetary policy raises rates and pulls money out of circulation to fight inflation — exactly what happened in 2022 to 2023, when the Fed pushed the federal funds rate from near zero above 5 percent within a year to cool the highest inflation in over forty years. Both directions carry real trade-offs: act too aggressively and risk a recession; act too slowly and risk runaway inflation, as happened during the stagflation of the 1970s.

Why the Whole World Watches the Fed

Because the dollar has been the world's primary reserve currency since the Bretton Woods agreements of 1944, Fed decisions reverberate globally. Higher U.S. rates strengthen the dollar, which raises repayment costs for countries holding dollar-denominated debt (Argentina, Turkey, and Sri Lanka have all faced this strain) and can trigger a balance-of-payments crisis requiring help from the International Monetary Fund. When the Fed eventually raises rates after a period of low rates, capital that flowed into emerging markets can rush back to the U.S., a pattern nicknamed the "taper tantrum." The Fed's mandate is officially domestic, but its actual reach is de facto global.

📚 KEY VOCABULARY

Recurring — happening repeatedly or over regular intervals.
Liquidity — the ease with which an asset can be quickly converted into cash.
Fragmented — broken into separate parts, lacking unity or coordination.
Money Supply — the total amount of money circulating in an economy.
Monetary Policy — how a central bank manages money supply and interest rates to influence the economy.
Turmoil — a state of great confusion, disorder, or instability.
Insolvency — the inability to pay debts when they are due.
Insulate — to protect from outside influence or impact.
Interest Rates — the cost of borrowing money, as a percentage of the loan.
Transparency — being open and clear so decisions can be understood by others.
Inflation — a general rise in prices that reduces the purchasing power of money.
Federal Funds Rate — the rate at which banks lend to each other overnight.
Open Market Operations — the central bank buying or selling government securities to influence the money supply.
Securities — financial instruments such as stocks or bonds.
Reserve Requirements — the percentage of deposits banks were once required to keep on hand rather than lend. Set to zero percent in March 2020.
Expansionary / Contractionary Monetary Policy — actions to increase money supply and lower rates to boost growth, or reduce money supply and raise rates to slow inflation.
Succession — one person or institution taking over a role after another.
Bretton Woods Agreements — 1944 agreements creating a global monetary system tied to the U.S. dollar; established the IMF and World Bank.
Reverberate — to spread widely and cause continuing effects.
Bonds — debt securities where investors lend money for regular interest and eventual repayment.
Balance-of-Payments — a record of all financial transactions between one country and the rest of the world.
International Monetary Fund (IMF) — a global organization providing financial assistance and guidance to countries in need.
Exports — goods and services produced in one country and sold to another.
Global Investment Flows — the movement of investment money across countries and markets.
Financial Outflow — money leaving a country or company, often from investment withdrawals.
De Facto — existing in practice, even if not officially recognized.
Backlash — a strong negative reaction to a policy or event.
Stagflation — high inflation and high unemployment occurring at the same time.
Globalization — the increasing interconnectedness of economies and societies worldwide.
Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) — digital forms of a country's official currency issued by its central bank.
Algorithmic Trading — computer programs automatically buying and selling assets based on set rules.
Mortgage-Backed Securities — investments made of bundled home loans.
Credit Default Swaps — financial contracts that act like insurance against a borrower's default.
