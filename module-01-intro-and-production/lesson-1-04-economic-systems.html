📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Who decides what gets made, how, and for whom?
Every society has land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship to work with, but not every society organizes them the same way. The framework a society uses to answer what, how, and for whom to produce is its economic system. There are four: traditional, command, market, and mixed.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe how each of the four economic systems answers what, how, and for whom to produce.
2
I will be able to weigh the strengths and weaknesses of each system.
3
I will be able to explain why nearly every modern nation runs a mixed economy.

Traditional Economy

The oldest and most basic system, rooted in custom and passed down through generations — farming, hunting, and craft skills stay within families, and production is aimed at subsistence rather than surplus, with barter replacing money for exchange. It offers strong social cohesion and cultural continuity, but little technological change and slow adaptation to outside pressure. Found today mainly in rural or remote indigenous communities.

Command Economy

The government makes the major decisions: what gets produced, how, and for whom, often through detailed quotas. Private property and enterprise are heavily restricted. Strengths: fast mobilization of resources for large projects and guaranteed basic services. Weaknesses: chronic inefficiency, shortages alongside surpluses, and restricted economic freedom, since planners cannot track real-time consumer demand. The Soviet Union industrialized rapidly this way but eventually collapsed under its own inefficiencies (1991); North Korea remains a rigid modern example.

Market Economy

Supply and demand, not government planners, set production and prices. Property rights and voluntary exchange are central; the price system coordinates millions of individual decisions, and competition drives quality and innovation. Strength: high personal freedom and social mobility. Weakness: market failures — monopolies, and externalities like pollution — that an unregulated market has no built-in way to correct.

Mixed Market Economy

Private enterprise still drives production, but government regulates, provides services the market undersupplies, and funds a social safety net. The United States blends free-market principles with regulation, labor law, public education, and programs like Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid; European nations like Sweden and Germany lean further toward social welfare, funded by higher taxes. Antitrust laws curb monopolies; environmental and safety rules address externalities. The hard part is that no one agrees exactly where the balance between freedom and regulation should sit — that debate is ongoing in every mixed economy, including ours.

📚 KEY VOCABULARY

Economic System — the structure by which a society organizes the production, distribution, and consumption of goods and services.
Apprenticeship — learning a trade through hands-on experience under a skilled worker.
Continuity — maintaining consistent practices or traditions over time without major interruption.
Subsistence — producing or using only enough to meet basic survival needs, without surplus.
Barter — the direct exchange of goods and services without using money.
Standardize — to establish uniform rules or measurements to ensure consistency.
Cohesion — the unity or bond that holds members of a group or society together.
Quota — a set limit or required amount, often applied to production, trade, or sales.
Enterprise — a business or organized economic activity aimed at producing goods or services.
Mobilize — to organize and deploy resources or people for a specific purpose.
Inefficiency — the failure to use resources in the best way, leading to waste or reduced output.
Stifle — to hold back or prevent the growth of something.
Chronic — persistent, long-lasting, or recurring over time.
Capitalist — someone who supports or participates in an economic system based on private ownership and profit.
Supply — the amount of a good or service producers are willing and able to offer at different prices.
Demand — the desire and ability of consumers to purchase goods or services at various prices.
Property Rights — the legal rights to own, control, and use resources, goods, or land.
Voluntary Exchange — buyers and sellers freely trading goods or services to their mutual benefit.
Social Mobility — the ability to move up or down within a society's economic or social hierarchy.
Monopoly — a market in which a single seller dominates and controls the supply of a good or service.
Externality — a side effect of economic activity that affects others not directly involved in the transaction.
Social Safety Net — government programs designed to protect people from economic hardship.
Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid — U.S. federal programs providing retirement/disability benefits, health coverage for seniors, and health coverage for low-income individuals, respectively.
Robust — strong, durable, able to perform well under different conditions.
Antitrust Laws — government regulations that prevent monopolies and promote fair competition.
Exploitation — the unfair or unethical use of people or resources for personal or economic gain.
