📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do people make decisions when they cannot have everything they want?
You already think like an economist more than you realize. Every time you choose one thing and give up another — a lunch, an evening, a purchase — you are wrestling with the problem this whole course is built on: resources are limited, wants are not. Economics is the discipline that studies how people, businesses, and governments handle that gap.

Scarcity: the Problem Every Society Must Solve

Economics is sometimes called the science of scarcity. Scarcity does not mean nothing is available — it means there is never enough of everything to satisfy every possible use for it. Farmland used to grow food cannot also grow biofuel; a forest logged for timber cannot also stay untouched wilderness. Because resources — tangible ones like land, money, and machinery, and intangible ones like time and skill — are limited, every decision involves a trade-off: choosing one option means giving up another.

Because scarcity is universal, every society, no matter its politics or geography, has to answer three questions: What should be produced? How should it be produced? For whom should it be produced? A market economy lets consumer demand and business competition answer those questions; a centrally planned economy has government officials answer them. Either way, the answers shape who has access to what, and how wealth moves through a society.

⚡ SORT IT · WHAT, HOW, OR FOR WHOM?
Every society has to answer three questions: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. Decide which question each decision answers.
1. A city decides to build a new school instead of a new stadium.
2. A farm switches from hand watering to drip irrigation to use less water.
3. A town gives free school lunches only to families who earn the least.
4. A bakery buys an automatic mixer so it needs fewer workers to make the same bread.

Two Ways to Zoom: Micro and Macro

Economics splits into two branches. Microeconomics zooms in on individual decision-makers — a household deciding what to buy, a worker deciding where to apply, a business deciding what to produce — and asks why ticket prices spike in peak season or how a sale changes buying habits. Macroeconomics zooms out to the whole economy — unemployment, inflation, growth, trade — and asks why an entire economy slides into a recession or grows quickly. You will use both lenses all semester: micro to explain one firm's decision, macro to explain the system those decisions add up to.

Worth noticing: economists lean on two more ideas constantly. Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative you gave up to make your choice. Incentives are the financial, social, or personal motivations that push people toward one choice over another. You will meet both terms again and again this semester — they are not one-lesson vocabulary, they are how economists think.

Match each situation to the concept it best illustrates.
