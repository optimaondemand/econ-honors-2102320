The Dust Bowl: Drought, Debt, and Foreclosure on the Plains
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What happens to a family when the land they own is blowing away and the bank still wants its payment?
On the southern Plains, the Depression came with a second disaster: the soil itself began to move. This lesson connects what you learned in 6.01 about debt and plowing to a real catastrophe. It also asks a hard question about property. When your land is your income, your home, and your security, what does it mean to lose it?

🌪️ PART 1 · THE DAY THE SKY TURNED BLACK
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

Black Sunday

On Sunday, April 14, 1935, a cold front pushed south across the Plains. It carried with it a wall of dust. The National Weather Service in Dodge City, Kansas, has kept the record. The dust cloud reached the city at 2:49 in the afternoon. It came from the north. It stood an estimated 500 to 600 feet tall and moved at 50 to 60 miles per hour. Almost total darkness followed at once.

The Weather Bureau log called the storm "one of terror." The storm stretched roughly 800 miles from central Nebraska to the Mexican border. People saw birds flying ahead of the cloud. Afterward, dead small birds were found on the ground. The Red Cross set up relief headquarters in Liberal, Kansas. Its officials counted 17 deaths in Kansas from dust pneumonia and three from dust suffocation that were tied to the storm.

That day gave the region its worst reputation. But it was one storm among many. The Plains had suffered dust storms for years by then. What was new about Black Sunday was how many people saw it.

The Scientist in the Capital

A soil scientist named Hugh Hammond Bennett had been warning anyone who would listen. In 1935, he was testifying to Congress about soil loss when a dust storm from the Plains reached Washington. Accounts differ on exact details of the scene, so treat the famous story with some care. What is well documented is the result. On April 27, 1935, Congress passed the Soil Conservation Act. It created the Soil Conservation Service, a permanent agency inside the Department of Agriculture, with Bennett as its first chief.

💡 DOES THIS SOUND FAMILIAR?
Notice the pattern. A scientist raises an alarm for years. Little happens. Then a dramatic event makes the problem impossible to ignore, and Congress acts within weeks. In your own life, you may have seen the same thing with a warning you ignored. Wisdom often looks like acting before the storm arrives, not after.
🌾 PART 2 · WHY THE SOIL BEGAN TO MOVE

Two Causes Working Together

The weather mattered. A serious drought began in 1931 on the southern Plains, according to American Heritage. Dry years are a normal part of life in the region. The native prairie had survived them for thousands of years.

The difference was what lay on top of the ground. In Lesson 6.01, you learned about the Great Plow-Up. Tractors and one-way plows had turned millions of acres of grassland into wheat fields. American Heritage reports that about 32 million acres of sod were broken in two decades. When rain stopped, the fields dried out, the roots that used to hold the soil were gone, and the wind could lift the topsoil and carry it away. That wearing away is called erosion.

Wheat prices fall and debts stay fixed.
➡
Farmers plow more land to earn more.
➡
Native grass, which held the soil, is gone.
➡
Drought comes. Dry soil has nothing to hold it.
➡
Wind lifts the topsoil. Fields and income are lost.

This chain is the heart of the lesson. Notice that no step needed a villain. Each farmer was trying to pay a bill and feed a family. The weather did the rest. This does not mean that choices did not matter. It means the harm came from many sensible decisions adding up in a way nobody had planned.

A Scientist's Warning, Seven Years Early

Here is where the primary source comes in. In April 1928, the U.S. Department of Agriculture published a circular, a short government report, titled Soil Erosion: A National Menace. It was written by H. H. Bennett and W. R. Chapline. Seven years before Black Sunday, it had already named the problem. Here is a passage from the end of Part 1, written by Bennett:

📜 PRIMARY SOURCE
"The writer, after 24 years spent in studying the soils of the United States, is of the opinion that soil erosion is the biggest problem confronting the farmers of the Nation over a tremendous part of its agricultural lands. It seems scarcely necessary to state the perfectly obvious fact that a very large part of this impoverishment and wastage has taken place since the clearing of the forests, the breaking of the prairie sod, and the overgrazing of pasture lands."
H. H. Bennett and W. R. Chapline, Soil Erosion: A National Menace, U.S. Department of Agriculture, Circular No. 33, April 1928.

Read the passage slowly. Bennett does not say the weather is the problem. He says the damage has "taken place since" the plowing and clearing. He is arguing that human choices created the vulnerability. He also writes without panic. His tone is that of a man who has spent 24 years looking at the same thing and is stating a conclusion.

Now read the passage a second time with three questions in mind. What is the author's evidence? He cites 24 years of study, which is a long record of experience and not a single event. Who is he writing for? A federal circular is meant for farmers and officials who can act. And what does he leave out? He does not say how a farmer should pay for new practices. That gap is where economics enters the story.

Ask yourself what a farmer reading this in 1928 might have done. Some farmers did change their practices. But a farmer with a mortgage payment due had strong reasons to keep plowing. That is the economic trap: the person who sees the danger may still be unable to afford the safe choice.

🏠 PART 3 · DEBT, PROPERTY, AND LOSING THE FARM

An Externality You Can Breathe

In Lesson 4.04 you learned that an externality is a cost that lands on someone who never agreed to it. The Dust Bowl is a vivid case. Dust from a stripped field did not stay on that farm. The Weather Bureau record shows a single storm reaching about 800 miles. A farmer who left a field bare was not only risking his own soil. He was adding to the dust that settled on his neighbor's land and, on the worst days, in his neighbor's lungs.

This helps explain why conservation became a government matter. One farmer who protects his soil gains little if the farms around him stay bare, because the wind does not respect a fence line. Protecting soil works best when many farmers do it together, and that is hard to arrange without a shared rule or shared support. The Soil Conservation Service was built to help organize that effort.

What Foreclosure Means

When you borrow money to buy a farm, the loan is usually a mortgage. The farm itself is the collateral, the property the lender is allowed to take. If you cannot make the payments, the lender can take the property and sell it. That is a foreclosure. The lender does not do this out of cruelty. The loan was made on those terms, and the lender needs to recover its money.

For a farm family, though, the farm is not only an asset. It is a home, a job, a way of life, and often several generations of work. Losing it means losing all of those at once. Economist Lee Alston, writing in the Journal of Economic History, found that farm foreclosures during the 1920s and 1930s reached heights never seen before or since. Remember that the 1920s were already in trouble before the Depression began.

Not every farmer owned the land. A tenant farmer rented land from an owner and paid in cash or a share of the crop. A tenant had no mortgage to lose, but also no ownership to protect. When crops failed, a tenant could lose the right to farm the land whenever the owner chose to end the arrangement. Owners, tenants, and hired hands each faced the same drought with very different amounts of protection.

The Arithmetic of a Bad Year

Here is a made-up farm, with simple numbers, to show how one dry year turns into a crisis. In a normal year, the farm sells $1,000 worth of wheat. Of that, $400 goes to the loan payment, $300 to seed and fuel, and $300 to feed and house the family. Everything is tight, but it works.

Try this in your notebook before you click: in a drought year, the farm harvests only one fifth of its normal crop, so it sells $200 of wheat. The costs stay the same. How large is the shortfall?
The farm needs $1,000 to cover its loan, its supplies, and its family. It has $200. The shortfall is $800. Notice that the loan payment alone is $400, twice the farm's whole income for the year. A farmer who cannot cover that gap must borrow more, sell equipment, or give up land. This is how a weather problem became a debt problem, and a debt problem became a foreclosure.

Property and the Free Society

Why does the idea of secure property matter so much in American thought? The founders believed that property, meaning your home, your land, and the fruits of your work, was a pillar of freedom. A person who owns something can say no to a boss, a landlord, or a ruler. A person with nothing has little to bargain with.

Economists make a related point. People work harder, invest more, and take better care of land they are sure they can keep. If property is not secure, those habits weaken. You will see this argument again in Module 7. The Dust Bowl shows the other side of it. Property rights are not only about the law. They also depend on whether a family can afford to hold on, and a farm that blows away is a farm that no deed can protect.

The Government Responds

The Soil Conservation Service was the answer to the dust. Conservation, the protection of natural resources like soil and water, became a national policy. Bennett's agency worked with farmers on practices meant to keep soil in place. The agency exists today under a new name, the Natural Resources Conservation Service, which Congress adopted in 1994.

But notice what conservation did not do. It did not erase anyone's debts. A farmer who had planted better and still owed the bank was still in debt. That problem would need a different set of tools, and in Lesson 6.05 you will see what the government tried.

Think like an economist about the choice to stay or go. A family deciding whether to stay faced costs on both sides. Staying meant risking another failed crop. Leaving meant giving up land, equipment, and neighbors, often with no promise of work elsewhere. There was also a trap that economists call the sunk-cost trap: the more you have already put into a farm, the harder it feels to walk away. But money already spent is gone either way, and a good decision looks forward. That is easy to say and very hard to do.

The Plains also forced hard choices on families. Some stayed and endured. Others left, often with very little, for places they hoped would offer work. Those who stayed and those who left were both making decisions with terrible information. If you take one idea from this lesson, take this: the Dust Bowl was caused by weather, by farming practices, and by debt at the same time. Remove any one of them, and the story changes.
