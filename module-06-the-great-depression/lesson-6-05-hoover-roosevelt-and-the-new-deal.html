📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two presidents faced the same disaster. Why did they choose such different tools?
By now you know how deep the hole was. This lesson asks what the government did about it. You will compare Herbert Hoover's choices with Franklin Roosevelt's, using the tools you learned in Modules 3, 4, and 5: tariffs, taxes, spending, the money supply, and the rules of the market. You will also meet some programs that tried to control prices and output, and ask whether controlling them was wise.

📚 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth a look before you read. Each one is bold and underlined in the text. Hover over it for a quick definition, or tap the speaker to hear it said.
Bank Holiday /bank HOL-ih-day/
A short period when the government orders banks to close so they can be checked and a panic can calm down.
Collective Bargaining /kuh-LEK-tiv BAR-gin-ing/
Workers joining together to negotiate with an employer as one group about pay and working conditions.
FDIC /ef-dee-eye-SEE/
The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, the government agency that insures bank deposits.
Foreclosure /for-KLOHZ-er/
When a lender takes and sells property, such as a farm or a house, because the borrower cannot pay the loan.
Price Floor /pryse flor/
A legal minimum price. Sellers may not charge less than it.
🏛️ PART 1 · HOOVER: THREE DECISIONS AND THEIR CRITICS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Which do you think is the better first response to a deep downturn? (No wrong answer. This is an argument economists still have.)

Decision One: The Tariff

In Module 5 you learned that a tariff is a tax on imports, and that tariffs can protect some domestic businesses while raising prices for buyers. In June 1930, President Hoover signed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act. It began as a bill to help farmers, and it grew until it raised duties on more than 20,000 imported goods.

Other countries answered with tariffs of their own. World trade shrank sharply in these years. Historians generally see the act as one of the things that made the Depression worse. How much worse is still debated, because trade was already collapsing for other reasons. A fair summary is this: it did not help, and it probably hurt.

Decision Two: The RFC

You met the Reconstruction Finance Corporation in Lesson 6.02. Congress created it in January 1932 to lend to banks, railroads, and other institutions in trouble. It was the most direct federal intervention of Hoover's term. Critics say it came late and was too small. Supporters say it set the pattern that Roosevelt followed.

Decision Three: The Tax Increase

This is the one that surprises many students. In 1932, Congress passed and Hoover signed the Revenue Act. It raised the top federal income tax rate from 25 percent to 63 percent and broadened who had to pay. Hoover believed that stability required a balanced budget, and tax collections were shrinking along with the economy.

Think about what you learned in Module 4. The usual advice in a recession is to cut taxes or increase spending so that people have more to spend. Raising taxes in the middle of a contraction does the opposite. Critics say the 1932 act made the downturn worse. Supporters of Hoover answer that he feared large deficits would scare lenders and make borrowing harder. Both sides had reasons. The same year, voters chose a new president.

A fair account also gives Hoover his due. The RFC was a large step for a president who distrusted federal power. And the belief that a government should balance its budget, even in hard times, was widely held in 1932. John Maynard Keynes's major book arguing the opposite did not appear until 1936. Hoover was not ignoring the textbook of his day. He was following it. Whether the textbook was right is exactly what the Depression put to the test.

💡 WHAT THE FED DID
Remember Lesson 6.02. Hoover did not control the Federal Reserve, which is a separate institution. In 1932, the Fed bought about $1 billion in government securities between April and August, and then reversed course. The monetary part of this story belongs to the Fed, not the White House.
📜 PART 2 · MARCH 1933: SPEAKING TO A FRIGHTENED COUNTRY

The Inaugural Address

Franklin D. Roosevelt took office on March 4, 1933. The banks were closing or about to close. Two of the sentences he spoke that day are among the best known in American politics. Here is the first.

📜 PRIMARY SOURCE
"So, first of all, let me assert my firm belief that the only thing we have to fear is fear itself--nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror which paralyzes needed efforts to convert retreat into advance."
Franklin D. Roosevelt, First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1933 (public domain; text from the Avalon Project, Yale Law School).

Notice what Roosevelt names as the enemy. It is not a person and not a policy. It is "nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror" that "paralyzes needed efforts." You just studied how a bank run works. In a run, fear is the cause. Roosevelt is making an economic claim, not only a speech: if people stop believing the banks are safe, the banks will fail no matter how sound they are.

📜 PRIMARY SOURCE
"Our greatest primary task is to put people to work."
Franklin D. Roosevelt, First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1933.

Fixing the Banks

Two days later, on Monday, March 6, Roosevelt declared a bank holiday. All banks closed. The Federal Reserve closed too. The point was to stop the runs and give examiners time to sort sound banks from broken ones.

On March 9, Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act, and Roosevelt signed it the same day. Many members of Congress had no time to read it before they voted. It gave the government power to inspect and reopen banks. On March 13, banks reopened in the twelve Federal Reserve cities. On March 14, banks in about 250 cities with clearing houses followed. On March 15, sound banks across the country reopened.

The president then did something new. On March 12, he gave the first of his Fireside Chats, a radio talk to the whole country. He told them: "I can assure you that it is safer to keep your money in a reopened bank than under the mattress." It worked. Currency held by the public had jumped by $1.78 billion in the four weeks before March 8. By the end of March, the public had put about two-thirds of that back into banks. On March 15, the first day the stock market reopened, the Dow rose 15.34 percent.

That is one of the clearest examples in the course of confidence acting as a real economic force. Nothing about the banks' assets changed in a few days. What changed was what people believed about them.

Insurance, So It Never Happens Again

In June 1933, Roosevelt signed the Banking Act of 1933, often called Glass-Steagall. It created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. The FDIC began insuring deposits on January 1, 1934, protecting up to $2,500 for each depositor. Congress raised that to $5,000 in July 1934. The FDIC says that at the start it guaranteed $11 billion in deposits.

This fixed the problem you studied in Lesson 6.02. If your deposit is guaranteed, you have no reason to rush to the bank. That removes the cause of a run. It is a good example of a rule that works because it makes a certain kind of panic unnecessary.

Insurance solves one problem and can create another. If depositors know their money is protected, they have less reason to check whether their bank is taking foolish risks, and a banker may feel freer to take them. Economists call this moral hazard: when people are shielded from the cost of a risk, they may take more of it. That is why deposit insurance comes with rules and inspections. A good policy has to ask not only whether it solves today's problem, but what incentives it creates for tomorrow.

Later in 1933 and into 1934, the country also moved away from the gold standard. The Federal Reserve's history lists the Gold Reserve Act of 1934 among the major reforms of this period. Freed from the gold link, the money supply could grow. You will see in the next lesson why many economists think this mattered more than almost anything else.

💲 PART 3 · CONTROLLING PRICES AND OUTPUT

The Idea Behind the AAA

Farm prices had fallen for years. You saw in Lessons 6.01 and 6.04 how debts stayed fixed while crop prices dropped. The Agricultural Adjustment Act, signed in May 1933, tried to raise prices by paying farmers to grow less. For cotton, the law allowed contracts under which farmers agreed to cut output at least 30 percent below the previous year. A tax on processors, such as the companies that ran cotton mills, paid for the subsidies.

Apply what you learned in Module 2. If supply falls, price tends to rise. That was the idea. The people who liked it said it would lift farm incomes, and that higher crop prices would make debts easier to pay and keep more families from foreclosure. The people who disliked it asked a hard question: in a country with hungry people, is it wise to pay farmers to produce less? And who pays? Processors and consumers pay more, and it is hard to say who gains in the end.

The Idea Behind the NIRA

The National Industrial Recovery Act became law on June 16, 1933. It created codes of fair competition for each industry. Representatives of each industry wrote a code, and the government approved it. A code had to set minimum wages and maximum hours. Industries that agreed to wage floors and to stop cutthroat price cutting were given freedom from antitrust laws. The government eventually approved 557 basic codes and 189 supplementary codes.

Section 7(a) of the act gave workers the right to join unions and take part in collective bargaining. That means bargaining as a group with the employer.

The reasoning behind it was a response to deflation. Prices and wages were falling, and when wages fall, buyers can buy less, which pushes prices down more. The NIRA tried to stop that spiral by setting a price floor under both wages and goods. Supporters hoped to stabilize the economy. Critics argued that setting prices by agreement can protect large firms and shut out small competitors, and that higher costs may keep businesses from hiring. Economists still argue about the net effect.

Four Levers

It helps to sort what you have just read into the kinds of tools a government can use. Trade policy changes the rules for goods that cross a border. Fiscal policy changes taxes and spending. Monetary policy changes the supply of money and the cost of borrowing. And regulation changes the rules that businesses and banks must follow. Most real policies mix more than one lever, but each has a main one. Try sorting the five below.

A Fair Comparison

Judge both presidents by the same standard. The first-order economic problem is to make and share enough that people live well. By that standard, neither man solved it in his time. Hoover made some choices, such as the tariff and the tax increase, that many economists believe made things worse. Roosevelt made choices, such as the bank rescue, that worked quickly. He also made choices, such as the NIRA, whose effects are much more debated.

Look at traits, not single acts. One fair way to describe Hoover is steady and cautious, with a firm hold on the idea of a balanced budget. When conditions changed, that steadiness could look like rigidity. Roosevelt was willing to act and to experiment under uncertainty. When it worked, as with the banks, it restored trust. When it did not, he risked committing to programs that could not last. Both traits are strengths in some conditions and weaknesses in others. That is what makes character such a hard thing to judge.
