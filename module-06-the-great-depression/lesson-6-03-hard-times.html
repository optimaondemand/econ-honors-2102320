📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What does it mean when one worker in four has no job?
Numbers can describe a disaster, but they cannot make you feel it. This lesson does both. You will learn how big the downturn was, how economists measure it, and what it looked like in a real place. Then you will ask who was supposed to help, and why help was so slow to arrive.

📊 PART 1 · THE SIZE OF THE DROP

A Tour of the Numbers

In 1929, only about 3 percent of the workforce was unemployed. By 1933, the most commonly cited estimate is about 25 percent. That is roughly one worker in every four, and about 12.8 million people by the figure kept at the FDR Presidential Library.

3% ➡ 25%
unemployment, 1929 to 1933 (the most commonly cited estimate)
More than a quarter
how far real output fell, 1929 to 1933 (exact figure depends on the data)
25% to 33%

About 9,000
banks that suspended operations, 1930 to 1933

Look at the last three tiles together. The economy was producing less, prices were sinking, and thousands of banks had closed. Any one of those would be a bad year. All of them at once, year after year, is what makes this the Great Depression.

Reading the Business Cycle at the Bottom

In Module 4 you learned the phases of the business cycle. The Depression is the most extreme example in American history. Economists who date the cycle place the peak in August 1929. The contraction lasted until March 1933, a total of 43 months. March 1933 is the trough, the lowest point.

Peak. August 1929. Output is at its highest.
➡
Contraction. 43 months of falling output, falling prices, and rising unemployment.
➡
Trough. March 1933. The lowest point, with the banking system in collapse.
➡
Expansion. 50 months of recovery, until a new peak in May 1937.

A normal recession lasts months. This one lasted more than three and a half years. A normal recession is also followed by a recovery that brings people back to work quickly. The recovery after 1933 was real, but it was long and uneven, and it stalled again in 1937. We will look at that in Lesson 6.06.

The Spiral

Why did the numbers keep getting worse for so long? One reason is that each loss caused the next one. Economists call this a spiral, and it looks like this.

Workers lose their jobs and spend less.
➡
Businesses sell less and cut their prices.
➡
Falling prices squeeze profits, so businesses cut costs.
➡
Cutting costs means laying off more workers.

Then the loop starts again from the top. This is why economists fear a steady fall in prices in a way they do not fear a small rise. When prices rise a little, people tend to spend. When prices are falling, people wait, because things will be cheaper next month. If everyone waits, sales fall further. Add the fixed debts from Lessons 6.01 and 6.02, and you can see why the spiral was so hard to stop.

Why There Is No Single Number

You might expect to find one clear unemployment rate for 1933. You will not. The 24.9 percent figure comes from an economist named Stanley Lebergott, who counted people on government work projects as unemployed. A different economist, Michael Darby, counted those workers as employed, and his figure for 1933 is about 20.6 percent. Neither one is making a mistake. They answer slightly different questions.

There is a second reason. Nobody was publishing a monthly national unemployment rate in the way the government does today. The numbers economists use now are reconstructions, built later from census records and other data. That is worth knowing. It means the people living through 1933 knew how bad things were in their own town, but not exactly how bad they were everywhere.

There is also a question of who counts. Today, a person is counted as unemployed only if they have no job and are looking for one. Someone who stops looking is not counted, even though they are out of work. Economists call this person a discouraged worker. In a deep downturn, some people stop looking because there is nothing to find. So even a careful count can miss part of the damage.

And the national figure hides big differences between places. In Utah, for example, unemployment reached 35.8 percent, the fourth highest rate in the nation, according to the state's history site. A town that depended on one mine or one factory could lose nearly everything at once.

This is a good place to notice honesty in scholarship. When two careful researchers disagree by several points, the right response is to report both and explain why. Either number tells the same story: a huge share of American workers had no regular job, and the loss was spread across the whole country.

Try this in your notebook before you click: a town has 4,000 workers. Using the 25 percent figure, how many are out of work? Now ask yourself how that number changes what a store owner, a landlord, and a school principal in that town can expect.
Out of 4,000 workers, 1,000 would have no job. The store owner would see customers disappear and prices sink. The landlord would see rent go unpaid. The school would see families unable to pay for books, shoes, and sometimes lunch. Each of those losses reduces someone else's income, which is how a job loss in one place spreads to many.

🏚️ PART 2 · WHAT HARD TIMES LOOKED LIKE

Hooverville

When people could no longer pay rent, some of them built their own homes out of what they could find. These camps of scrap wood, tin, and cardboard were called shantytowns. Across the country, people named them Hoovervilles, after President Herbert Hoover, whom many blamed for the Depression.

The name had a political origin. According to HistoryLink, the Washington State history site, the term first appeared in print in 1930. Charles Michelson, the publicity director of the Democratic National Committee, used it in a New York Times article about a homeless camp in Chicago. It was a partisan label from the start. Whether the blame was fair is a question for Lesson 6.05. For now, notice that people were already naming their misery after a person.

One of the best documented was in Seattle. It began in October 1931, when about twenty men, led by an unemployed logger named Jesse Jackson, built shacks on a vacant lot that had once been a shipyard. It grew to as many as 1,200 people. At one point, Seattle had eight shantytowns. City officials tried twice to tear the shacks down, and each time the residents built them again. Over time, residents dug into the ground and built roofs of tin and steel to make them last.

Read that once more. These were not people who had given up. They were building, repairing, and rebuilding. They organized themselves into something like a community because they had nowhere else to go. This is perseverance, and it is worth saying clearly. It is also a sign of a system that had failed them.

Not Everyone Lost the Same Amount

A fair account has to say that the Depression did not hit everyone equally. A worker with a steady paycheck found that falling prices stretched the same wages further. A family with money in a bank that stayed open was in much better shape than a family whose bank had closed. A farm family with no debt was better off than one that owed the bank.

That is not a defense of the period. It is a reminder to ask a good economic question: what were people's starting positions? Debt, savings, and type of work made a big difference. The people who were hurt most had the least cushion to begin with, and they had the fewest ways to protect themselves.

🤝 PART 3 · WHO WAS SUPPOSED TO HELP?

A System Built for Small Problems

Before the 1930s, most help for poor and jobless people came from local sources. Local governments, churches, and charities ran soup kitchens and gave food and shelter. This is relief. It is how Americans had handled hard times in the past, and for ordinary downturns it was often enough.

A depression is not an ordinary downturn. Local charities and churches ran out of money at the same moment that more families needed help than ever before. Many cities and states were close to bankruptcy from trying to cope. Their own tax income was falling, because people with no jobs paid no taxes. The institutions that were supposed to catch people could not catch this many.

What about the federal government? In this period, federal help for relief arrived mostly as loans. Under President Hoover, the Reconstruction Finance Corporation lent money to states so they could run their own relief programs. A loan has to be repaid, and states with falling incomes did not want to take on more debt. That gap led to the next step. In May 1933, soon after Franklin Roosevelt took office, Congress created the Federal Emergency Relief Administration. It gave grants to states and cities instead of loans. According to the Living New Deal project, it distributed about $3.1 billion between May 1933 and December 1935. Harry Hopkins ran it.

Handouts and Work

There is an old argument about the best form of help. Some people worry that giving money for nothing can wear down a person's habit of working. Others say that no one can search for a job on an empty stomach. Many officials came to prefer work relief, which pays unemployed people to do useful jobs on public projects. Work relief keeps a worker's skills and dignity, and it leaves something behind, such as a road or a school.

President Roosevelt put it directly in his First Inaugural Address on March 4, 1933. He said, "Our greatest primary task is to put people to work." You will read more of that speech in Lesson 6.05. The idea fits a deeper American belief that a job is more than income. It is a place in the community.

💡 LOOK AHEAD
In 1935, Congress passed the Social Security Act, which included a system of unemployment insurance. That is something your family can rely on today. Before it, nothing like it existed at the national level. You will study what it covered, and who it left out, in Lesson 6.06.

A Question of Responsibility

Americans had long believed that help should start close to home. Your family comes first, then your neighbors, your church, your town, your state, and only last the nation. There is wisdom in that order. People closest to a problem know the most about it, and help given by a neighbor comes with a face and a name.

The Depression tested how far that idea could stretch. When the problem is too big for the nearest circle, the next circle has to step in. The hard question is when. Move too soon, and you weaken the habit of looking after one another. Move too late, and families go hungry while the institutions closest to them run dry. Reasonable people drew that line in different places in 1932, and they still do.

What the Lesson Leaves You With

The first-order economic problem of any society is to produce and share enough that most people live decently. In 1933, the American economy was failing that test. Many people who were willing and able to work could not find any, and the old ways of helping had been outrun by the size of the problem.

A thoughtful student does not leap straight to blaming one person. Hoover and Roosevelt both faced a situation with few good options and many unknowns. The question to carry into the next lessons is more useful: what tools did leaders have, which ones did they use, and what did those tools actually do?
