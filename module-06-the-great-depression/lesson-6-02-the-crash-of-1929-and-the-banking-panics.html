📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A stock market can fall without ruining a country. So how did this one fall into a depression?
Most people remember October 1929. Fewer remember that the worst of the Depression came later, when the banking system itself began to fail. This lesson follows the money. You will see how a loss on paper became a loss of money in circulation, and why economists still argue about who could have stopped it.

📉 PART 1 · OCTOBER 1929: THE FALL OF THE DOW
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When you hear "the Great Depression," what do you picture first? (No wrong answer.)

A Peak, Then a Slide

The Dow Jones Industrial Average is a measure of stock prices. It closed at 381.17 on September 3, 1929. That was the top. Then prices began to slip, and many of the people who had bought stock on margin started to feel the trapdoor from Lesson 6.01.

On Monday, October 28, the Dow fell nearly 13 percent in one day. On Tuesday, October 29, it dropped nearly 12 percent more. That second day came to be called Black Tuesday. By mid-November, the Dow had lost almost half its value. It kept falling for years. On July 8, 1932, it closed at 41.22, which was 89 percent below the peak. It did not climb back to its 1929 high until November 23, 1954.

Those are the numbers the Federal Reserve's own history reports. They are worth sitting with. A person who put $1,000 into the average stock in September 1929 would have seen it shrink to roughly $110 by the summer of 1932. That is a very hard thing to live through.

Why a Crash Is Not Automatically a Depression

Here is the puzzle. Stock prices have fallen sharply before, and the country did not sink into a decade of misery. A stock crash destroys wealth that exists on paper. Some people lose their savings. Others stop spending because they are afraid. That hurts, but it does not by itself shut down factories and banks.

Something more has to happen. The economy has to lose its ability to lend, to pay, and to move money from one hand to another. That is what separates a bad year from a catastrophe.

One more fact complicates the story. Economists who date the business cycle put the peak of the economy in August 1929, which is two months before the crash. The economy had already started to turn down. The crash did not start the downturn from a standing start. It landed on an economy that was already slowing.

What the Fed Did in October

In the first panic, the New York Fed acted. It bought government securities on the open market, made it easier for banks to borrow, and lowered the discount rate. It told the big banks it would supply the reserves they needed. Those were good instincts, and they kept the first wave from spreading right away. The harder test came later.

🏦 PART 2 · WHEN BANKS RUN OUT OF TRUST

How a Bank Can Fail With Honest Management

In Module 3 you learned that banks do not keep all your deposits in a vault. They lend most of it out. This is how banks earn money and how businesses get loans. It works as long as depositors do not all ask for their money on the same day.

When they do, the bank has a problem. This is a bank run. Even a sound bank cannot hand back everyone's money at once, because it has lent it. If you hear that your bank is in trouble, the smart move for you is to get your money out now. But if everyone does that, the bank collapses. Your fear is reasonable and it also helps cause the disaster. That is the trap.

Today, you are protected from this. If your bank fails, the government repays you up to a set amount, which is deposit insurance. In 1930 that protection did not exist. A family that lost its bank lost its savings. Everybody knew it, and everybody acted on it.

A Bank Run in Numbers

Here is a simple model. A small bank holds $100 in deposits. It keeps $10 in cash and lends out $90. In an ordinary week, a few customers withdraw and a few deposit, and the $10 is plenty. Now suppose 20 percent of depositors want their money on the same day. That is $20. The bank has $10. It can try to call in its loans, but borrowers cannot repay overnight. It can try to sell its loans, but if every bank is selling at once, buyers offer very little. So the bank closes, even though many of its loans may be good ones.

The lesson is that a bank can be sound and still run out of cash at the moment people demand it. When it closes, the harm spreads beyond its depositors. A business that counted on a loan from that bank cannot get one, so it cuts jobs. Those workers spend less, so other businesses sell less. That is how one failure becomes many.

Remember the farmer with the $1,000 debt in Lesson 6.01. When prices fell by a quarter or more across the whole economy, millions of borrowers, not only farmers, found their fixed debts growing heavier. That made loans harder to repay, which made banks weaker, which made runs more likely. The loop closed on itself.

The Panics Begin

The Federal Reserve's history gives the first dates. On November 7, 1930, the Bank of Tennessee in Nashville closed its doors. It was a major part of a company called Caldwell and Company. Within days, related banks in Knoxville and Louisville failed too. On December 11, 1930, the Bank of United States closed. It was the fourth-largest bank in New York City.

"Within a few weeks, hundreds of banks suspended operations," the Fed's account says. About a third of them reopened within a few months. Most were closed for good. Fear spread faster than facts. A depositor in one town read about a failure in another and went to check on a bank that was perfectly healthy.

The Size of the Collapse

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation reports that from 1930 to 1933, approximately 9,000 banks suspended operations. For comparison, there were fewer than 25,000 banks in the whole country, so this was a very large share of them.

The money supply is the total amount of money people can spend. A big part of it is not paper cash. It is the money sitting in checking accounts. When a bank closes, that money stops working. And when frightened people pull cash out of banks and stuff it in a mattress, the banking system has less to lend. The Fed's history says the money supply fell by nearly 30 percent from the fall of 1930 to the winter of 1933. Prices fell by about the same proportion. Other measures show a drop of roughly a quarter to a third, depending on which prices you count.

💡 THE FED WAS MEANT TO PREVENT THIS
In Lesson 3.02 you learned that a central bank can act as a lender of last resort, lending to solvent banks in a panic so they can meet withdrawals. That was the Federal Reserve's job. As you will see below, how well it did that job in the early 1930s is one of the biggest arguments in American economic history.
🪙 PART 3 · GOLD, MONEY, AND THE QUESTION OF BLAME

The Gold Standard

In 1929, the dollar was on the gold standard. A dollar could be traded for a fixed amount of gold, and the Fed had to hold enough gold to back the money it issued. Many countries did the same. That tied their economies together. If one country raised interest rates to keep its gold, others felt the pull.

The Fed's history says the international gold standard linked interest rates and monetary policy among the countries that used it. That is one reason the Depression became worldwide. It is also why the Fed's choices in 1931 look so troubling to many historians.

On September 21, 1931, Great Britain left the gold standard. Foreign holders of dollars began converting them into gold, and gold flowed out of the United States. In early October, the New York Fed responded by raising its discount rate. That made borrowing more expensive in the middle of a banking crisis. The gold drain stopped by late October. But bank failures kept going. Some Fed leaders, the history says, preferred to defend the gold standard over helping banks that were failing.

Put yourself in the place of a Fed official in October 1931. Gold is leaving the country. If it keeps leaving, the Fed may not have enough to meet its legal requirement. Raising interest rates is the textbook way to attract gold. But banks are failing, and higher rates make borrowing harder for them. You can protect the gold or you can protect the banks. Which would you choose, and what would you need to know first? There is no neat answer. That is what makes it a real dilemma, and not a mistake that was obvious to everyone at the time.

Was It Too Little, Too Late?

The Fed's own history is blunt about its record. It says the Fed's most serious failure was its "failure to stem the decline in the supply of money." Fed leaders disagreed about how to respond, and the history judges their efforts "too little and too late."

There were attempts. Congress created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, known as the RFC, in January 1932 to lend to banks and railroads. Between April and August of 1932, the Fed bought about $1 billion in government securities. Bank failures eased in the first half of that year. Then they rose again, especially in the Midwest and West, and a national panic followed in early 1933. In spring 1932, the Fed expanded aggressively, then reversed course. A different pattern of choices might have made a difference. That is contingency again.

Two Ways of Reading the Same Story

Economists do not agree on how to weigh the causes. Here are two views, stated fairly.

One view focuses on money. Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz argued that the Fed let the money supply collapse, and that this turned an ordinary recession into a depression. A more recent study by Kris Mitchener and Gary Richardson found that money supply and bank lending fell by more than 30 percent between 1929 and 1932, and that much of the drop followed depositor runs. On this view, the crash was a spark, and the banking collapse was the fire.

Another view focuses on spending and confidence. When households and businesses became afraid, they stopped buying and investing, and an economy that does not spend cannot employ people. You met this argument from John Maynard Keynes in Lesson 5.05. Many economists today combine both views. They see the money collapse and the collapse in spending as one linked disaster.

The honest conclusion is that this is still debated, and we should not pretend otherwise. What nobody debates is the result. According to the Federal Reserve, the Depression hit bottom in March 1933, when the commercial banking system collapsed. Real output, the total amount the country produced, fell by more than a quarter between 1929 and 1933, though the exact figure depends on whose data you use. That is the setting for the next lesson, which asks what life was like at the bottom.
