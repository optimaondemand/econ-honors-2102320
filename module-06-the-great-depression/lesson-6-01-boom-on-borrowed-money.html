Boom on Borrowed Money: The 1920s, Farm Debt, and the Great Plow-Up
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Was the crash of 1929 already built into the boom that came before it?
The 1920s felt like the start of a new age. Cars, radios, and stock tickers promised that prosperity was now permanent. This lesson looks under the paint. You will see how borrowing held up the boom, why farmers were left out of it, and why a drought that had not yet arrived was already waiting for the Great Plains.

Collateral /kuh-LAT-er-ul/
Something of value that a borrower promises to give up if the loan is not paid back.
Discount Rate /DIS-kount rayt/
The interest rate the Federal Reserve charges banks when they borrow from it.
Gold Standard /gohld STAN-derd/
A system in which a country promises to exchange its paper money for a fixed amount of gold.
Installment Credit /in-STAWL-ment KRED-it/
Buying something now and paying for it in regular payments over time.
Margin (buying on margin) /MAR-jin/
Buying stock with borrowed money, so that you put down only part of the price yourself.
📚 PART 1 · A COUNTRY THAT LEARNED TO BUY TOMORROW TODAY
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think about the last time you bought something big, or watched a family member do it. Did it come with a payment plan, a loan, or a credit card? (No wrong answer.)

The Boom Was Real

Start with what was true. By the end of the 1920s, the American economy was producing more than it ever had. Factories turned out cars and radios. Cities built skyscrapers. In 1929, unemployment was only about 3 percent. Most workers who wanted a job had one.

That matters, because it would be easy to treat the 1920s as a fake. They were not. People really did live better than their parents had. The question is not whether the boom was real. The question is what held it up, and how strong those supports were.

How Households Paid for the Boom

Many of the new goods cost more than a family could save in a few months. A car or a radio could be a year of careful saving, or more. So sellers offered another way. Buy now, take it home today, and pay a little each month. This is called installment credit.

Installment credit is not evil. It is a tool. A family that buys a car on payments may get to work and earn more. But the tool has a rule built into it. Every payment you owe next year comes out of next year's income. If that income stops, the payments do not stop. They just pile up.

In the 1920s, millions of households took on payments like these at the same time. As long as jobs held and paychecks kept coming, the system looked solid. Nobody had tested what would happen if the paychecks shrank. That was the weak spot. It would stay hidden until something pressed on it.

Economists often separate two kinds of borrowing. When a business borrows to buy a machine, the machine can earn the income that pays the loan back. When a household borrows to buy something it will use up, the payments must come out of income earned somewhere else. Neither kind is wrong. But an economy with a lot of the second kind is more exposed when incomes drop. In the 1920s there was plenty of both, and in any single case it is not always easy to tell which was which.

📈 PART 2 · THE STOCK MARKET AND THE TEN PERCENT DOWN PAYMENT

Buying Stock With Borrowed Money

Borrowing did not stop at cars. It reached the stock market too. Prices of stocks climbed through the 1920s, and more and more people wanted a piece of it. The Federal Reserve's own history of this period describes how people bought stock on margin. A buyer typically put down about 10 percent of the price and borrowed the rest. The stock itself served as collateral for the loan. If the buyer could not repay, the lender kept the stock.

The Fed's history also notes that other lenders put "increasing sums" into loans to brokers. In other words, the more stock prices rose, the more money flowed in to lend for buying stock. That fed the rise. And the rise made the loans look safer. It was a loop.

Why Ten Percent Down Is a Dangerous Number

Here is a simple example. Imagine you want $1,000 worth of stock. You put down $100 of your own money and borrow $900. Now watch what happens when the price moves.

If the stock price...Your stock is worth...Your own $100 becomes...
rises 10 percent$1,100$200. You doubled your money.
stays the same$1,000$100. You owe the loan, but you are even.
falls 10 percent$900$0. Your money is gone, and the stock is worth exactly what you owe.

A 10 percent rise doubled your money. A 10 percent fall erased it. That is why margin buying felt like magic on the way up and like a trapdoor on the way down. The same borrowing that multiplied the gains multiplied the losses by the same amount.

Try this in your notebook before you click: what happens to the $1,000 example if the stock price falls 20 percent? Work out what the stock is worth and what you still owe.
The stock is now worth $800. You still owe the lender $900. You have lost your whole $100 and the stock no longer covers the loan. A lender in that spot would demand more money from you right away, which was called a margin call. If you could not pay it, the lender sold your stock to get its money back. Millions of small sales like that could push prices down further.

The Fed Faced a Hard Choice

The leaders of the Federal Reserve saw the speculation, and they did not agree on what to do about it. In February 1929, the Federal Reserve Board wrote that the Federal Reserve Act did not "contemplate the use of the resources of the Federal Reserve Banks for the creation or extension of speculative credit." The Board wanted to pressure member banks directly not to lend to stock speculators.

The New York Fed, led by its governor George Harrison, wanted something different. It wanted to raise the discount rate. That is the interest rate the Fed charges banks that borrow from it. The Board turned down several of New York's requests. In August 1929, it finally gave in, and the New York discount rate reached 6 percent.

Both sides had a point. A higher rate makes all borrowing cost more, not just stock borrowing, so it can slow honest businesses along with the gamblers. Direct pressure is hard to carry out when banks can find other ways to lend. The Fed's history also says that rate increases in 1928 and 1929 slowed the economy. Because the United States was on the gold standard, that slowdown spread to other countries too. Hold onto that idea. It returns in the next lesson.

🌾 PART 3 · THE FARM WAS NOT INVITED

A Boom That Skipped the Countryside

While cities boomed, many farmers were already in trouble. Farm prices dropped sharply in the autumn of 1920, and they did not bounce back the way other prices did. Many farmers had borrowed money to buy land or equipment when prices were high. Their loans stayed the same size while the money they earned from crops shrank.

This is the first place in the story where debt meets falling prices. It will not be the last. When you owe a fixed number of dollars and the dollars you earn get smaller, every payment weighs more. An economist named Lee Alston studied the record and found that the number of farms lost to lenders in the 1920s and 1930s reached levels never seen before or since. Notice that the 1920s are in that sentence. The farm crisis started before the crash.

A Fixed Debt in a World of Falling Prices

Here is the idea in numbers. Suppose a farmer owes the bank $1,000, and in a normal year her grain sells for $1,000. One year of crops clears the debt. Now suppose prices fall by a quarter. The same crop sells for $750. The debt did not shrink. It is still $1,000. She now needs about a third more grain to pay the same bill, because $1,000 divided by $750 is about 1.33. Nothing about the farmer changed. The prices did.

That is why economists worry about borrowers when prices fall. A fixed debt gets heavier in real terms as prices drop. Keep this example in your head. In the next lesson, prices fall across the whole economy by a quarter or more, and millions of borrowers face the same problem at once.

The Great Plow-Up

The southern Plains of Kansas, Oklahoma, Texas, Colorado, and New Mexico were covered by native grass. Its roots were thick and deep. They held the soil in place through droughts and through wind. Then the machines arrived.

PBS's American Experience describes the difference. With a team of horses, a farmer could barely turn three acres of prairie sod in a day. With a tractor, he could plow fifty. Add the one-way disc plow and the combine, and a family could farm land that would have been impossible a generation earlier. A historian writing in American Heritage reports that wheat acreage rose about 200 percent between 1925 and 1931, and in many counties by 400 to 1,000 percent.

Machines let one farmer plow far more land.
➡
More wheat reached the market, and wheat prices fell.
➡
To earn the same income, farmers plowed even more land.
➡
Native grass, which had held the soil for centuries, was gone.

The Kansas Historical Society says it plainly: the new machines raised productivity, but that caused prices to fall, and farmers made up for it by breaking even more land for wheat. Look at the logic from a farmer's side. Every single decision made sense. A lower price meant you needed more bushels to pay the bank. More plowing meant more bushels. Nothing about it was foolish.

Now look from the side of the whole Plains. A thousand farmers made the same sensible choice at once. The result was more wheat than the market wanted and soil with nothing holding it down. You studied supply and price signals in Module 2. Here is a case where the signal said "plow more" to every farmer, and nobody was in charge of adding up what that meant. It took years to find out.

💡 DID YOU NOTICE?
Both of the weak spots in this lesson, the borrowed stock money and the borrowed farm money, have the same shape. People took on fixed debts, counting on a future that kept rising. Neither group was acting out of wickedness. They were responding to the incentives in front of them. That is exactly why economists study incentives so carefully.
🧭 PART 4 · THE BOOM HAD MORE THAN ONE POSSIBLE ENDING

Nothing Here Was Inevitable

It is tempting to read the 1920s backward. We know the crash is coming, so every sign of trouble looks like a flashing light. But the people living in 1927 or 1928 did not have our map. They saw a prosperous country with a few soft spots, and they argued about what to do.

A careful historian looks for the forks in the road. The Fed leaders disagreed about whether to raise rates or lean on banks directly. Different choices would have produced a different 1929. Lenders could have required more than 10 percent down. Farmers could have kept more of the grass. Prices of land and wheat could have steadied. None of these things was certain to happen, and none was impossible.

This is called contingency, and it is one of the most important habits of mind in history. It means that the past was open when it was the present. Real people made real choices, and different choices were available. If you remember nothing else from this lesson, remember that the Great Depression was not scheduled in advance. It grew out of decisions, and decisions can be studied.

A Question of Habits

There is also a question about character. The founders of the United States believed that a free people needed certain habits to stay free, and one of them was self-government: the ability to restrain your own appetites. A nation that borrows more every year to buy what it cannot yet afford is testing that habit at a very large scale.

Be careful with this thought. It does not mean that every borrower in the 1920s was greedy. A farmer who borrowed to feed his family was not a speculator. It means that when habits of prudence weaken across a whole society, the economy has less room to absorb a shock. Whether that was the main cause of the Depression is something economists still argue about. We will return to that debate honestly in Lesson 6.06.
