The Legacy: Who Was Helped, Who Was Left Out, and How the Depression Ended

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do you judge a set of policies when the experts still disagree about what worked?
Every explanation of the Depression has to deal with an uncomfortable fact: it did not end the day the New Deal began. This lesson looks at who the new programs protected and who they did not, how the courts limited them, why the economy fell again in 1937, and what economists think finally ended the slump. It asks you to hold two things together, confidence in what is known and honesty about what is not.

🛡️ PART 1 · THE SAFETY NET AND ITS HOLES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When you judge a big policy, which question do you ask first? (No wrong answer.)

A New Kind of Promise

In 1935, Congress passed the Social Security Act. It set up old-age insurance for retired workers and created a national system of unemployment insurance. In Lesson 6.03 you saw that before this, jobless Americans had no national program to turn to. A worker now earned protection as part of the job, and the idea that the national government had a role in a person's security was no longer unusual. This is the system that later became the social safety net you studied in Module 1.

But the law did not cover everyone. According to the Social Security Administration, it covered wage and salary workers under age 65 who worked in industry and commerce. Left out were agricultural laborers, domestic servants in private homes, and several other categories. Think about how many people that was. In 1935, a large share of the workforce, including a majority of Black workers, worked on farms or in other people's homes.

Why Were They Left Out? Two Readings

Historians have long debated the reason, and it deserves a careful look at both sides.

The first reading comes from the Social Security Administration's own historian, Larry DeWitt. He studied the record and concluded that the exclusion came from questions of administrative feasibility. Farm and household workers were hard to track, and the new payroll tax had to be collected from employers. In his judgment, there is no evidence of an additional policy motive involving racial bias.

The second reading points to the result. Agricultural workers and household servants made up a majority of the African American workforce in 1935. Whatever the motive, many scholars note that excluding them meant that most Black workers were left out of old-age insurance and unemployment insurance when the law began.

These two views are not as far apart as they first look. One is about why Congress did what it did. The other is about what happened as a result. A careful student can accept that the law was shaped by practical limits, and also that its effect fell heavily on those workers. If a historian tells you the matter is simple, ask for the evidence.

The same question of who bears the cost applies to the farm program you met in Lesson 6.05. The AAA's subsidies were paid for by a tax on processors, such as the owners of cotton mills. A tax like that does not always stay with the person who writes the check. A processor can try to pass it on in higher prices, or to pay farmers less. Who ends up paying is not decided by the law that names the taxpayer. It is decided by how buyers and sellers respond.

💡 WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ECONOMICS
Every program draws a line around who is covered. Where the line falls changes who benefits, who pays, and who must find another way. Economists call this the question of incidence. It is one of the most useful ideas in public policy, and it applies as much to a tax or a tariff as to a pension.
⚖️ PART 2 · THE COURTS AND A SECOND DOWNTURN

The Supreme Court Steps In

Two of the biggest 1933 programs did not survive. On May 27, 1935, the Supreme Court ruled in A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States that part of the National Industrial Recovery Act was unconstitutional. That means the Court found it was not allowed by the Constitution. The Court held that the law gave too much lawmaking power to the executive branch, and it said the Constitution did not give Congress authority over a small New York business like Schechter's. The vote was unanimous. Chief Justice Hughes wrote the opinion.

On January 6, 1936, in United States v. Butler, the Court struck down the tax that paid for the Agricultural Adjustment Act's subsidies. The administration had expected the AAA to survive because it was voluntary, with farmers signing contracts. It did not.

Notice the rule at work. In the American system, the courts can check even popular programs. Some people saw the Court as a roadblock on the way to recovery. Others saw it as the Constitution doing its job. Whichever view you take, the rulings mattered. The NIRA and the AAA, the programs built on price floors and output limits, did not continue in their original form.

The Recession Inside the Depression

By 1937, the economy had grown for more than four years. Then it turned down again. The business cycle dates show a new peak in May 1937 and a trough in June 1938, a contraction that lasted 13 months. Industrial production fell by roughly 30 percent, according to one study by the National Bureau of Economic Research. Real output fell by about 10 to 11 percent. Unemployment rose again, though the sources give very different numbers, so we will not quote one.

What caused it? Economists disagree. One argument points to the Federal Reserve. In 1936 and early 1937, because banks were holding a lot of extra reserves, the Fed doubled the minimum reserves banks had to hold. Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz argued that this forced banks to cut back and made the money supply shrink. Other economists, including Charles Calomiris, Joseph Mason, and David Wheelock, studied bank records and found that the new rules did not bind most banks, so the effect may have been small. The cause of the 1937 recession is still not settled.

The lesson is a hard one. A recovery that looks secure can reverse. The people living through 1937 had just begun to hope, and then they watched the numbers turn around.

🔍 PART 3 · HOW DID IT END, AND WHAT DO WE MAKE OF IT?

Three Explanations

Economists give three main answers to the question of what ended the Depression. They are not completely separate, and each has serious defenders.

ExplanationThe argumentA caution
MoneyThe economist Christina Romer found that nearly all the recovery before 1942 came from monetary expansion. Gold flowing into the United States swelled the money supply, which lowered real interest rates and encouraged spending on investment and on durable goods like cars.It is one economist's analysis. It does not mean spending did nothing.
Government spendingUnder the Keynesian view you met in Lesson 5.05, government spending can fill the gap when private spending falls.Romer concluded that fiscal policy contributed almost nothing before 1942. Critics at the Levy Institute argue that the usual measures of fiscal effects may understate it.
War mobilizationThe United States entered World War II in December 1941. Military production and the draft changed the labor market in ways peacetime policy had not.This lies mostly after the period Romer studied. It raises the harder question of whether war is a good model for peacetime policy.

Why would gold coming in help? Once the dollar was no longer tied tightly to gold, incoming gold could expand the money supply. More money in the banking system meant banks could lend at lower rates. Lower real interest rates make borrowing to build a factory or buy a car look cheaper. That is the chain Romer describes: money, then interest rates, then spending. You do not have to accept her weights to notice the chain.

The point of the table is not to choose a winner. It is to show you how a careful person holds a question that remains open. You can be confident of what is documented. The recession dates, the money supply, the programs, and the court rulings are all part of the record. You can also be honest that the weights to give each cause remain in dispute.

The Counterfactual Problem

To say a policy worked, you need to know what would have happened without it. That is the hard part, because history runs only once. We cannot go back, remove the FDIC, and watch a second 1933. Economists get around this by comparing. They compare one country with another, one region with another, or one year with a similar year. Each comparison has weaknesses, because no two cases are exactly alike.

So when you read a claim such as "the New Deal ended the Depression" or "the New Deal prolonged it," ask one question first: compared with what? A claim without a comparison is only an opinion. A claim with a careful comparison is the beginning of an argument you can test.

Judging the New Deal Fairly

The first-order economic problem is to produce and share enough that the whole population lives well. Measured that way, here is a fair summary. The bank rescue and the FDIC worked quickly, and deposit insurance has lasted for generations. The expansion that began in March 1933 ran for 50 months. Many programs helped real families. Some of the most ambitious ones, the NIRA and the AAA in their original form, were struck down or later reshaped. The economy did not fully recover in the 1930s, and it fell back in 1937. And the safety net, when it first appeared, left out many of the workers who were most exposed.

Notice that this summary does not call the New Deal a triumph or a failure. It reports what happened. That is what honesty in scholarship looks like, and it takes more courage than taking a side. Some readers will want a verdict. A good economist, or a good citizen, learns to say: here is what the evidence supports, here is where it runs out.

What the Depression Taught

Look back across the six lessons. Debt made the boom fragile. A stock fall was turned into a banking crisis by fear and by a shrinking money supply. People suffered most where they had the least cushion. A government that began with loans and tax increases changed course. Programs that tried to control prices met resistance in the courts. And a recovery that seemed secure stalled when policy turned.

Notice also what kept reappearing. Incentives shaped behavior. Money and credit moved through the whole economy. Trust was a real economic asset. Policy involved real tradeoffs, and different people paid different costs. Those are the central ideas of this course. The Depression is the largest laboratory in American history for testing them.

You are joining a conversation that began in the 1930s and still goes on. Keynes, Friedman and Schwartz, and Romer are not museum pieces. Their arguments are used today in central banks and in Congress. When you take a side on a question like this, and defend it with evidence, you are doing what economists do.

Two very different subjects come next. In Module 7, you will study a response to economic problems that rejected markets altogether, and you will ask how it worked in practice. In Module 8, you will move from the whole economy to your own, and learn how money, loans, and interest rates work when you are the one borrowing.
