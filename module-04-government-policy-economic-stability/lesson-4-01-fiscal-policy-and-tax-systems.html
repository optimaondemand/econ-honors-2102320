Expansionary vs. Contractionary Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy works through aggregate demand: government spending injects purchasing power; taxes remove it. Expansionary fiscal policy (tax cuts, more spending) fights recessions and high unemployment — FDR's New Deal public-works programs during the Great Depression, or the 2020-2021 COVID-19 stimulus checks and Paycheck Protection Program loans, are two examples separated by nearly a century but built on the same logic. Contractionary fiscal policy (higher taxes, less spending) fights inflation when demand outpaces what the economy can produce, as debated during the Vietnam-era spending surge of the late 1960s. Timing is the hard part: acting too late lets a problem worsen; acting too aggressively can trigger the opposite problem.

Three Goals of Fiscal Policy

Price stability keeps inflation and deflation both in check. Full employment doesn't mean zero unemployment; it means nearly everyone who wants a job at the going wage can find one — the 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, an $831 billion package after the 2008 financial crisis, aimed directly at this goal. Sustainable growth comes from long-term investment in things the private sector underfunds on its own: the Eisenhower-era interstate highway system reduced transportation costs and spurred suburbanization for decades; the same logic applies to education and R&D spending today. The trade-off across all three goals is public debt — borrow too much to fund these goals and risk crowding out private investment; underspend in a crisis and risk a longer recession.

How Taxes Are Structured

Direct taxes (income tax, corporate profit tax) are paid straight by the earner. Indirect taxes (sales tax, excise duties) are levied on goods and passed to the consumer at purchase. Separately, taxes vary by progressivity: a progressive tax system (the U.S. federal income tax, with rising tax brackets) takes a higher percentage from higher earners; a proportional tax system (a flat tax) takes the same percentage from everyone; a regressive tax system (sales taxes, since everyone pays the same rate regardless of income) ends up taking a bigger bite out of lower incomes relative to what they earn. Most real tax systems, including the U.S., blend all three: progressive federal income tax alongside more regressive state sales and property taxes.

Historical case: Reagan-era tax cuts lowered the top marginal income tax rate from 70% in 1981 to 28% by 1986. Supporters credit the cuts with spurring investment; critics point to rising deficits and argue the benefits skewed toward the wealthy. Both claims are part of the same ongoing debate over how tax structure trades off growth against fairness.

📚 KEY VOCABULARY

Fiscal Policy — the government's use of taxation and spending to influence the economy.
Disposable Income — the money households have left to spend or save after taxes.
Aggregate Demand — the total demand for goods and services in an economy at a given price level.
Expansionary / Contractionary Fiscal Policy — increasing spending or cutting taxes to stimulate growth, or the reverse to slow an overheating economy.
Stimulus — government actions, such as spending or tax cuts, designed to boost activity during a slowdown.
Curb — to restrain or control something, such as inflation.
Deflation — a sustained decrease in the general price level.
Full Employment — a condition where nearly everyone who wants a job at prevailing wages can find one.
Accrue — to build up or accumulate over time.
Suburbanization — the growth of residential communities outside cities.
Public Debt — the total amount a government owes to creditors from borrowing.
Income Tax — a direct charge on individual or household earnings.
Levy — to impose or collect a tax, fee, or fine.
Sales Tax — an indirect charge added to the price of goods and services at purchase.
Excise Duties — taxes on specific goods, such as alcohol, tobacco, or gasoline.
Progressivity — the degree to which tax rates increase as income rises.
Progressive / Proportional / Regressive Tax System — a structure taking a higher percentage from high earners; the same percentage from everyone; or a larger effective share from low earners.
Tax Brackets — the income ranges that determine the rate applied in a progressive system.
Property Tax — a charge based on the value of land and buildings owned.
Deficit — the shortfall when government spending exceeds revenue.
Capital Gains Tax — a charge on profit earned from selling assets like stocks or real estate.
