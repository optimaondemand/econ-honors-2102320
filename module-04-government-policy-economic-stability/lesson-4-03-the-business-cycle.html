📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why does the economy never just stay good?
Fiscal and monetary policy don't operate in a vacuum — they're constant responses to a natural rhythm economists call the business cycle: peak, contraction, trough, expansion, repeat.

📚 PART 1 · FOUR PHASES, ONE RECURRING PATTERN

The Four Phases

At the peak, production and employment sit near their maximum, but demand outpacing supply creates bottlenecks and inflationary pressure — the housing peak before 2008 is a textbook example of a precarious high point. Contraction follows once demand slows: layoffs rise, spending falls, and a recession is technically two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth (the Great Depression and the 2007-2009 Great Recession are the starkest U.S. examples). The trough is the low point, where GDP is stagnant but excesses from the previous expansion finally clear — 2009 is the trough that set up over a decade of subsequent growth. Expansion is the recovery: GDP rises, unemployment falls, confidence returns, as it did from 2009 to early 2020, the longest U.S. expansion on record. Eventually expansion runs hot enough to create new inflationary pressure, and the cycle turns toward another peak.

Inflation Does Not Hit Everyone the Same Way

Inflation erodes the real value of money if wages don't keep pace. It hits fixed-income households hardest: retirees relying on private annuities without automatic cost-of-living increases lose purchasing power steadily, even though Social Security itself does adjust. It changes saving behavior, pushing money toward equities or other assets expected to outpace inflation, which can fuel speculative bubbles like the pre-2008 housing market. It creates uncertainty for businesses trying to plan when costs fluctuate erratically. And it favors debtors over lenders: someone repaying a fixed-rate mortgage during high inflation is effectively repaying less in real terms than they borrowed, while the lender loses purchasing power on what's repaid.

⚡ COMMIT, THEN CHECK · WHO COMES OUT AHEAD?
During a stretch of high inflation, a family keeps making the same fixed-rate mortgage payment every month. Who comes out ahead, the family or the lender?
In your notebook, pick one and explain why in a sentence. Then click here to check your thinking.
The family. Inflation means each dollar buys less, so the fixed payments are worth less in real terms than the money the family borrowed. The lender loses purchasing power on what is repaid. Inflation tends to favor debtors over lenders.

When Inflation Breaks: Hyperinflation

The Federal Reserve generally targets about 2% annual inflation as healthy. Hyperinflation is a different order of magnitude entirely: in 1920s Weimar Germany, prices doubled every few days; in 2000s Zimbabwe, a 100-trillion-dollar banknote became nearly worthless. In both cases, savings evaporated, wages could not keep pace, and people resorted to bartering or foreign currency because their own money had lost credibility. The U.S. faced a milder but still severe version in the early 1980s, when Federal Reserve Chairman Paul Volcker raised interest rates above 15% to break double-digit inflation — a policy that caused a sharp recession but restored long-term price stability.

📚 KEY VOCABULARY

Business Cycle — the recurring pattern of economic growth and decline through expansion, peak, contraction, and trough.
Downturn — a period when economic activity slows.
Bottleneck — a constraint in the production or supply process that limits output.
Precarious — an unstable or risky economic situation that could easily worsen.
Quarter — a three-month period used to measure economic performance.
Stagnant — a condition with little or no growth in output, jobs, or income.
Real Value — the purchasing power of money after adjusting for inflation.
Annuities — financial products providing regular fixed payments, often for retirement.
Equities — shares of ownership in a company.
Speculative Money — capital invested in high-risk assets hoping for large but uncertain returns.
Erratically — in an unpredictable or irregular manner.
Fixed-Rate Mortgage — a home loan where the interest rate stays the same throughout repayment.
Hyperinflation — an extreme, rapid rise in prices that severely erodes the value of money.
Bartering — the direct exchange of goods or services without money.
