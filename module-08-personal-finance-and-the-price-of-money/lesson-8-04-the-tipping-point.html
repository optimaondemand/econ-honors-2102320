📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If the payment never changes, when does the loan start working for you instead of for the lender?
You now know how to find a payment and open it up. This lesson asks the practical questions. When does most of each payment finally go to the loan itself? What happens to the total cost if you choose a shorter term, a larger down payment, or an extra payment now and then? And how do you put it all into a plan for a major purchase?
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to find the tipping point of a loan and explain why early payments are mostly interest.
2
I will be able to compare the total cost of a loan under different choices: term, down payment, and extra payments.
3
I will be able to build a short plan for financing a major purchase, using the total cost of credit.
📚 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth a look before you read. Each one is bold and underlined in the text. Hover over it for a quick definition, or tap the speaker to hear it said.
Loan Estimate /lohn ES-tuh-mit/
A three-page form a lender must give you after you apply for a mortgage. It shows the loan's key terms and costs so you can compare offers.
Principal /PRIN-suh-pul/
The amount of money you borrowed and still owe, not counting interest.
Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI) /PRY-vit MOR-gij in-SHUR-uns/
Insurance that protects the lender, not you, if you stop paying. Lenders often require it when your down payment is small.
Refinance /ree-FY-nans/
To replace an existing loan with a new loan, usually to get a lower rate or different terms.
Tipping Point /TIP-ing point/
In a loan, the payment number after which more of each payment goes to principal than to interest.
Total Cost of Credit /TOH-tul kost uv KRED-it/
Everything you pay for the privilege of borrowing: the interest, plus fees and required insurance, not counting the amount you borrowed.

Now answer the opening question. After 15 years, the balance is $103,865.60. The family has paid off $162,000 minus $103,865.60, which is $58,134.40, or about 35.9 percent of the loan, even though they have used half the time. After five years, the balance is $146,227.42, about 90.3 percent of the original loan. The loan is front-loaded with interest. That is not a trick. It is simply what happens when interest is charged on the balance you still owe.

'Does the schedule hold up? A good habit is to check a result by a second method. There is a closed-form formula for the balance after k payments, and it gives the balance after 60 payments as $146,227.41. The month-by-month schedule gave $146,227.42. The two differ by about a cent, because the schedule rounds every payment to the nearest cent and the formula does not. Two different methods agreeing to within pennies is a good reason to trust the table. If they had differed by hundreds of dollars, the right move would be to look for a mistake.

This matters when life changes. Families sell homes and cars much sooner than the end of the loan. If you sell after five years, you will still owe about nine-tenths of what you borrowed, and the sale price has to cover that before you keep any profit. Understanding the shape of the schedule keeps you from being surprised.

Look at the pattern. A shorter loan raises the payment but cuts the total interest sharply, because you owe the balance for fewer months. A bigger down payment lowers both the payment and the interest, since you are borrowing less. And a larger down payment may also remove the need for mortgage insurance, which in the sample costs $82 a month, although lenders set their own rules about that.

'Rate matters just as much as the term. If the same family had found a rate one full percentage point lower, 2.875 percent, the payment would have been $672.13 instead of $761.78, and the total interest would have been $79,967 instead of $112,241. That is a difference of about $32,274 over the life of the loan. This is why Lessons 8.01 and 8.02 mattered. A rate is not just a number on a form. It is thousands of dollars.

💡 A TOOL FOR CHECKING YOUR WORK
A spreadsheet can do all of this arithmetic for you, and it will not get tired. In Excel, the function PMT takes the monthly rate, the number of payments, and the amount borrowed, and returns the payment. Use it to check the numbers on this page, then build the full schedule one row at a time. A calculator you built yourself is the best way to understand what the formula is doing.

Paying a little extra

Extra payments go straight to principal, which is why they are so powerful early in a loan. Suppose the family makes one extra payment of $761.78 each year. The loan is paid off in 313 months, which is 26 years and 1 month, instead of 360, and the total interest falls by about $16,430. Suppose instead they add $100 to every payment. The loan is paid off in 290 months, and they save about $24,576 in interest.

Try this in your notebook before you click: if a family adds $100 to every monthly payment and the loan is paid off after 290 months instead of 360, how many months, and how many years and months, does that save?
360 minus 290 is 70 months. Seventy months is 5 years and 10 months. A small monthly habit shortens a thirty-year loan by almost six years. Check your own loan's terms first. The sample Loan Estimate has a prepayment penalty during the first two years, so a borrower should know exactly what the lender counts as a penalty before sending extra money.

One more thing a careful borrower does is shop. The same family, with the same credit and the same down payment, can be offered different rates and different fees by different lenders. A difference of one quarter of a percentage point on a loan this size can be worth thousands of dollars over its life. The few hours it takes to collect two or three Loan Estimates may be the best-paid hours of the whole process.

Refinancing

To refinance is to replace your loan with a new one, usually to get a lower rate. It is not free. The new loan has its own costs. A simple test is the break-even point: divide the cost of refinancing by what you save each month. Say, as a made-up case, that a refinance costs $3,000 and cuts the payment by $60 a month. Then it takes 50 months, a little over four years, to earn back the cost. If you expect to move or pay off the loan sooner than that, you lose money by refinancing.

📝 PART 3 · A CREDIT PLAN FOR A MAJOR PURCHASE

Plans beat hopes. Before you borrow for something large, such as a car, a first apartment's furniture, a computer for a business, or a home, you should write down a short plan. Here is the order that careful borrowers follow. The steps do not guarantee a good result, but they make a bad surprise far less likely.

StepThe question to answerWhere you learned it
1. PurposeIs this a need or a want, and what happens if I wait?Module 1
2. Price and down paymentHow much can I put down, and how much will I need to borrow?Lesson 8.03
3. RateWhat is my credit history, and what offers can I get from at least two lenders?Lessons 8.01 and 8.03
4. TermHow long will I be paying, and what does that do to the total cost?This lesson
5. Total cost of creditWhat will I pay beyond the price, once interest and fees are counted?This lesson
6. BudgetCan I make the payment each month and still handle surprises?Lesson 3.04
7. RisksWhat if my income falls, or the rate on a variable loan rises?Lesson 8.02

'Here is a smaller example, with made-up numbers, to practice the plan. A student wants to buy a used car and needs to borrow $20,000 at 6 percent. She compares a 48-month loan with a 60-month loan. The 48-month payment is $469.70 and the total interest is $2,545.60. The 60-month payment is $386.66 and the total interest is $3,199.60. The longer loan is easier on the monthly budget. It also costs $654.00 more in interest, and it keeps her in debt for another year, while the car loses value. Neither choice is simply right. The plan makes the tradeoff visible, so that she chooses it, rather than drifting into it.

Step five deserves a number. The total cost of credit is everything you pay for the privilege of borrowing, apart from the amount you borrowed. For the sample loan, add up what the Loan Estimate and our schedule show. Interest over 30 years is $112,240.80. Loan costs on the form are $5,672. Mortgage insurance, at $82 a month for 84 months, is $6,888.00. The total is about $124,801. Some costs on the form, such as property taxes and homeowner's insurance, are real but are not costs of credit, so we have left them out. Decide what belongs in the total by asking one question: would I pay this if I did not need to borrow?

That figure is almost as large as the price of the home. It does not mean that borrowing was a mistake. For most families, a loan is the only way to buy a home at all, and a home can be a sound investment and a place to build a life. It means the borrower should know the number, and should choose the loan with open eyes. A careful borrower asks not only "Can I afford the payment?" but also "What am I paying for the privilege of borrowing?"
