📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When you deposit your money in a bank, where does it go, and who decides what it costs to borrow it back?
You have learned that banks lend out your deposits, and that the Federal Reserve steers interest rates. This lesson connects the two. You will see how a bank can lend most of what you put in, how that creates risk and money at the same time, and how a vote in Washington reaches the cost of your own loan.

📚 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth a look before you read. Each one is bold and underlined in the text. Hover over it for a quick definition, or tap the speaker to hear it said.
Benchmark /BENCH-mark/
A standard that other things are measured against. A benchmark interest rate is a rate that other rates are compared with.
FOMC /eff-oh-em-SEE/
The Federal Open Market Committee. This group inside the Federal Reserve meets about eight times a year and votes on the target range for the federal funds rate.
Fractional-Reserve Banking /FRAK-shuh-nul rih-ZURV BANK-ing/
A system in which banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend out the rest.
Reserves /rih-ZURVZ/
Money that a bank keeps on hand or deposits at the Federal Reserve instead of lending out.
Target Range /TAR-get raynj/
The span of interest rates, such as 1/4 to 1/2 percent, that the Fed aims to keep the federal funds rate within.
Treasury Security /TREZH-uh-ree sih-KYOOR-ih-tee/
A loan to the U.S. government. You hand over money now, and the government promises to pay it back, usually with interest.
🏦 PART 1 · WHERE YOUR DEPOSIT GOES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
You put $1,000 in a bank. If every customer asked for their money back on the same day, could the bank hand it all over? (No wrong answer. You may change your mind.)

A bank is a middleman. Savers give it money, and borrowers pay it interest to use that money. The bank earns the difference. For that to work, it cannot leave your $1,000 sitting in a vault. It keeps a small part as reserves, money on hand or at the Federal Reserve for daily withdrawals, and lends out most of the rest. That arrangement is called fractional-reserve banking, because only a fraction of deposits is held back.

It is a good bargain for most people. A saver gets a safe place for money and some interest. A family gets a mortgage. A baker gets a loan for an oven. Without lending, those deposits would just sit still. But the arrangement has a built-in weakness. A bank can pay back depositors who ask for money on a normal day. It cannot pay all of them at once, because most of the money is out in loans.

Notice how much rests on trust. You trust the bank to be careful with money that is yours. The bank trusts borrowers to repay. Everyone trusts that everyone else will not panic. That chain of promises is why banks are among the most carefully regulated businesses in the economy. It also explains why a banker who lends carelessly is doing more than making a bad business choice. They are failing in a duty of stewardship toward their depositors.

You saw this in Lesson 6.02. When people fear a bank will fail, they hurry to withdraw their money. That is a bank run, and it can ruin a healthy bank, since even a sound bank cannot turn loans back into cash in an afternoon. Part of the answer was deposit insurance, created by the FDIC in 1933, which protects insured deposits up to a limit. If you know your money is safe, you have much less reason to run.

🔄 PART 2 · HOW LENDING CREATES MONEY, AND WHAT CHANGED IN 2020

Here is the textbook story, with a made-up reserve ratio of 10 percent. You deposit $1,000. The bank keeps $100 and lends $900 to a borrower. The borrower spends it, and the seller puts it in a bank. That bank keeps $90 and lends $810. The cycle goes on, and each round adds new deposits.

RoundNew depositTotal deposits so far
1$1,000.00$1,000.00
2$900.00$1,900.00
3$810.00$2,710.00
4$729.00$3,439.00
5$656.10$4,095.10
10$387.42$6,513.22

Each round is 90 percent of the one before, so the total keeps growing but at a slowing pace. In the limit, a $1,000 deposit supports up to $10,000 in total deposits, which is $1,000 divided by 0.10. The lesson economists draw is that lending creates money. The new deposits are real spending power that did not exist before the loans were made.

Try this in your notebook before you click: if the reserve ratio were 20 percent instead of 10 percent, what is the most that a $1,000 deposit could support?
1,000 divided by 0.20 is $5,000. A higher reserve ratio holds back more of each deposit, so each round of lending is smaller and the total is lower.
💡 REMEMBER FROM MODULE 3
In Module 3, you met reserve requirements as one of the Fed's tools. Here is where that tool stands today. On March 15, 2020, the Fed's Board announced that it was reducing reserve requirement ratios to zero percent, effective March 26, 2020. In the Board's words, this followed "the shift to an ample reserves regime." So today the law does not set a minimum fraction that banks must hold. The textbook story above still shows how lending creates deposits, but it is not the rule that limits banks now.

If a required ratio no longer holds lending back, what does? Economists point to several things. Banks must meet capital rules that require them to hold enough of their own money to absorb losses. Banks must also find borrowers who will take loans, and they must judge who will repay. And the Fed's own rate decisions change what lending costs. That last piece is the subject of the rest of the lesson.

Some people ask why banks do not simply hold every dollar of deposits in reserve and avoid runs altogether. They could. Such a bank would be safe, but it would also be a warehouse, not a lender. It could not fund a mortgage or a new oven, and it would have to charge depositors fees to stay in business. Fractional reserves are a tradeoff: you give up some safety to get lending, and you add deposit insurance and regulation to hold the risk in check. Economists argue about where the best balance lies.

📜 PART 3 · HOW THE FED SETS THE PRICE OF MONEY

Money, like anything else, has a price: the interest rate. The Federal Reserve cannot set every rate in the economy. But it sets one at the center, and the rest respond. The rate is the federal funds rate, which is what banks charge each other for overnight loans of reserves. You learned in Module 3 that it ripples out to other rates. The committee that decides is the FOMC, the Federal Open Market Committee. It votes on a target range, such as 1/4 to 1/2 percent, and the Fed uses its tools to keep the actual rate inside that range.

Two tools do most of the work today. The first is open market operations: buying or selling Treasury securities, which you studied last lesson. The second is the interest the Fed pays banks on the reserves they keep at the Fed. A bank has little reason to lend reserves to another bank for less than the Fed pays it to leave them there, so that rate acts as a floor. When the Fed raises it, other short-term rates tend to follow.

Congress gave the Fed two main goals, often called its dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices. In Module 3 you learned that the Fed aims for inflation of about 2 percent a year. The two goals can pull against each other. Lowering rates helps employment by making borrowing cheaper, but it can push prices up. Raising rates cools prices but can slow hiring. The committee has to weigh these, and it must do so with imperfect information. The phrase "ample reserves regime" in the 2020 announcement means that the banking system holds so many reserves that the Fed steers rates with the interest it pays, not by limiting how many reserves exist.

On March 16, 2022, the FOMC announced a decision, and the Federal Reserve's own statement is a primary source worth reading closely. The Fed had held its rate range near zero since March 2020, in response to the pandemic. This is the decision that ended that period. Read the main sentence, and the sentence that follows it.

📜 PRIMARY SOURCE
"In support of these goals, the Committee decided to raise the target range for the federal funds rate to 1/4 to 1/2 percent and anticipates that ongoing increases in the target range will be appropriate."

"Inflation remains elevated, reflecting supply and demand imbalances related to the pandemic, higher energy prices, and broader price pressures."
Federal Reserve, "Federal Reserve issues FOMC statement," March 16, 2022. federalreserve.gov. Ellipses and trimming are minor; check against the full release.

Notice three things. First, the Committee names its goals, which are maximum employment and stable prices. Second, it says more increases are expected, and that signal shapes what banks and borrowers do before anything else happens. Third, the vote. Eight members voted in favor, and one, James Bullard, voted against, because he "preferred at this meeting to raise the target range for the federal funds rate by 0.5 percentage point to 1/2 to 3/4 percent." He did not oppose raising rates. He wanted a bigger step. Even the experts who run the system disagreed about how fast to go.

That disagreement is a useful lesson in honesty about economics. Nobody knows exactly how strongly a rate change will affect prices and jobs, or how long the effect will take. The Fed acts on its best judgment and then adjusts. If a source tells you that a policy will work exactly as planned, treat it with caution.

💲 PART 4 · FROM A VOTE IN WASHINGTON TO YOUR MONTHLY PAYMENT
The FOMC raises its target range.
➡
Banks pay more to get short-term money, and they earn more on reserves.
➡
Banks raise the rates they charge borrowers.
➡
Borrowing costs more, so some people borrow and spend less.
➡
Spending cools, which tends to ease price increases.

Follow the chain to your own pocket. Suppose a family borrows $162,000 for 30 years at a fixed rate, the sample loan you will study in Lesson 8.03. Here is what the monthly payment is at four different rates. Each payment comes from the standard loan formula, which you will learn next lesson.

Interest rateMonthly payment (principal and interest)Total interest over 30 years
2.875%$672.13about $79,970
3.875%$761.78about $112,240
4.875%$857.32about $146,640
5.875%$958.29about $182,980

Look at the pattern. Each full percentage point adds roughly $90 to $100 to the monthly payment, and tens of thousands of dollars to the total cost. A rate decision that looks small in the news, one quarter of a point or half a point, is large when it is multiplied across a lifetime of borrowing. That is also why the Fed's choices are hard. A higher rate fights rising prices, but it also makes homes, cars, and business loans more expensive.

One more practical point. Many credit card rates and some other loans have variable rates, which move up and down with a benchmark, and so they respond quickly when the Fed changes course. A fixed-rate mortgage, by contrast, locks in the rate you were given at the start. That is one reason a fixed rate can feel safer, and also why the timing of when you borrow matters.

A good way to test your understanding is to ask a counterfactual question, the kind economists use. Suppose the Fed in March 2022 had left rates near zero and said nothing about future increases. Would borrowers have borrowed more? Would prices have risen more or less? There is no way to run the experiment again, and honest economists give different answers. What you can do is notice the logic on each side and weigh the evidence you see. That habit, reasoning carefully about what did not happen, is part of what an educated person does when reading about any policy.
