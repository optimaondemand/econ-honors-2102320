Treasury Securities: The Government's Borrowing and the Benchmark for Every Other Interest Rate

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When the government needs to borrow, who lends to it, and why does the rate it pays shape the price of everyone else's loan?
Every interest rate you will ever pay, on a car, a house, or a credit card, begins somewhere. In this lesson you will meet the loan that sits at the base of the whole system: the one the United States government takes out from investors. Understanding it will help you see why some loans cost more than others.

📚 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth a look before you read. Each one is bold and underlined in the text. Hover over it for a quick definition, or tap the speaker to hear it said.
Benchmark /BENCH-mark/
A standard that other things are measured against. A benchmark interest rate is a rate that other rates are compared with.
Face Value /fays VAL-yoo/
The amount a bond or bill promises to pay back when it comes due.
Maturity /muh-CHOOR-ih-tee/
The date when a loan or bond comes due and the borrower must pay it back in full. It also means the length of time until that date.
Treasury Security /TREZH-uh-ree sih-KYOOR-ih-tee/
A loan to the U.S. government. You hand over money now, and the government promises to pay it back, usually with interest.
Yield /yeeld/
The return you earn on a bill or bond, written as a percent of what you paid for it.
🏛️ PART 1 · THE GOVERNMENT AS BORROWER
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A stranger offers to borrow $1,000 from you for one year. Another borrower is the United States government. Both promise to pay you back. Which would you lend to first? (No wrong answer. You may change your mind.)

In Module 4, you learned that a government runs a deficit when it spends more than it collects in taxes. To cover the gap, it borrows. It does this by selling Treasury securities. When you buy one, you are lending money to the government. The government promises to repay you on a set date and, in most cases, to pay interest along the way. The securities are sold through public auctions, where investors bid for them, so the price is set by buyers and sellers, not by a government order.

Who buys them? Almost everyone with money to lend. Households buy them, and so do banks, pension funds, and investors in other countries. The Federal Reserve holds some as well, which is a story for the next lesson. For all of these buyers, the appeal is the same: a loan to the government is a way to store money safely and earn something for waiting. The government's side of the bargain is different. It gets cash today to pay for roads, soldiers, schools, and everything else in the budget, and it promises to pay in the future.

Treasury securities come in several forms. The main difference is how long you lend. The length of time until a security is paid back is called its maturity. TreasuryDirect, the Treasury's own website, lists the main types.

TypeMaturityHow you are paid
Treasury bill4 weeks up to 52 weeksNo regular interest. You buy it for less than its face value and are paid the full face value at maturity.
Treasury note2, 3, 5, 7, or 10 yearsInterest every six months, and the face value at maturity.
Treasury bond20 or 30 yearsInterest every six months, and the face value at maturity.
TIPS (inflation-protected)5, 10, or 30 yearsInterest every six months. The principal rises or falls with the Consumer Price Index.
Floating rate note2 yearsInterest every three months. The rate moves with short-term bill rates.

Notice that the bill is the odd one out. It pays no interest checks. Instead, you pay less than the face value today and receive the full face value later. The difference between the two is your earnings. That makes a bill a good place to start, because the arithmetic is simple.

📐 PART 2 · PRICE AND YIELD MOVE OPPOSITE WAYS

Here is a made-up example. These numbers are for practice only and are not today's market prices. Suppose a one-year Treasury bill has a face value of $1,000. An investor buys it for $960. After one year, the government pays $1,000. The investor has earned $40 on $960 of lent money. As a percentage, that is 40 divided by 960, which is about 4.17 percent. That percentage is the yield.

Try this in your notebook before you click: a different investor pays $980 for the same one-year bill with a $1,000 face value. How much do they earn, and what is the yield?
The investor earns $1,000 minus $980, which is $20. The yield is 20 divided by 980, which is about 2.04 percent. Notice that the investor who paid more earned a lower yield, even though the government pays the same $1,000 to both.
Price paid for a $1,000 one-year billDollars earnedYield
$980$202.04%
$960$404.17%
$940$606.38%
$920$808.70%

Look down the table. As the price paid falls, the yield rises. As the price rises, the yield falls. The seesaw works the same way for notes and bonds, although the math is a bit longer. This is one of the most useful facts in finance, and it is simple logic: the payment at the end is fixed, so a lower price means a bigger return on the money you put in.

Notes and bonds work a little differently, because they pay interest as they go. Suppose, as another made-up case, that a ten-year note has a face value of $1,000 and pays 4 percent a year, which is $40, delivered as two payments of $20. Those payments are fixed when the note is sold. Now imagine that a year later, new notes are being sold that pay 5 percent. Would you pay full price for the old 4 percent note? Probably not. To compete with the new notes, the old one has to be sold for less. Its price falls, and the yield for the person who buys it rises. The seesaw works here too.

Why would the price ever change? Because many buyers and sellers are trading. If investors suddenly want safety, they bid up the price of Treasury securities, which pushes the yield down. If investors prefer other investments, or worry that prices in the economy will rise, they pay less for Treasuries, which pushes the yield up. In short, the yield is the market's running answer to the question: what rate do I need to lend to the government?

⚖️ PART 3 · WHY ONE RATE BECOMES THE BENCHMARK

Investors treat Treasury securities as among the safest investments in the world, because they are backed by the taxing power of the U.S. government. That makes the Treasury yield a kind of floor. If you can earn a given return by lending to the government at very low risk, you will not lend to anyone riskier for the same return. Anyone else who wants to borrow has to offer more.

That is why the Treasury yield is called a benchmark. Other rates are built on top of it. A bank deciding what to charge for a mortgage, a company pricing a bond, and a lender setting a rate on a car loan all start by looking at what the government pays for money of a similar maturity. Then they add something for risk. Mortgage rates and long-term Treasury yields tend to move in the same direction over time, which is why news stories about "rising rates" often start with the Treasury market.

Economists describe the extra amount a lender asks for taking risk as a risk premium. It is the price of uncertainty. The idea is simple: if there is a real chance you will not be repaid, you will need to be paid more when you are.

Here is how a lender might build a rate, using made-up numbers. Start with the benchmark, say 4.0 percent for a loan of about the right length. Add 0.5 percentage points because the loan runs for many years and a lot can change. Add 1.0 percentage point because the borrower's credit history shows a few late payments. The lender now asks for 5.5 percent. A borrower with a spotless history would see the second add-on shrink or disappear, and would pay less on the same loan.

Try this in your notebook before you click: using the same benchmark of 4.0 percent, a 0.5 point term add-on, and a 2.0 point credit add-on for a very risky borrower, what rate would the lender ask? How much more is that than the first borrower's 5.5 percent?
4.0 plus 0.5 plus 2.0 is 6.5 percent. That is 1.0 percentage point more than 5.5 percent. On a $162,000 loan over 30 years, you will see in Lesson 8.03 that a full point can add about $95 to the monthly payment, which adds up to tens of thousands of dollars over the life of the loan.

Risk rises from left to right, and so does the interest rate a lender asks. The chart shows order, not exact numbers.

This ladder is why your own credit matters. In Lesson 3.04 you learned that a good credit history lowers the rate a lender offers. Now you can see the reason. The lender starts at the benchmark, then adds a premium that matches how risky you look. A strong history of repaying on time shrinks the premium. A weak one grows it.

Risk and return, in plain terms

Return is what you earn on money you lend or invest. Risk is the chance that you earn less than you hoped, or lose the money. These two ideas travel together, and no clever investor has found a reliable way to separate them. An offer that promises a high return with no risk should make you suspicious. Honesty about this tradeoff is a mark of a trustworthy adviser, and a mark of a trustworthy person managing their own money.

💡 PART 4 · WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU AND FOR THE COUNTRY

Return to the deficit. When the government borrows more, it must sell more securities, and it has to pay interest on them. Over time that interest becomes a cost in the budget, which is one reason economists worry about a large national debt. Whether that worry is large or small depends on how big the debt is, what the borrowed money bought, and what interest rates are. Reasonable people disagree on how much weight to give each of these. You studied the debate in Lesson 4.02, and now you know the mechanism behind it.

You also have a personal stake. The rate you pay for a loan is built in layers. At the base is a benchmark that responds to what investors expect from the economy. On top of that sits your own risk premium, which depends on you. You cannot control the benchmark, but you can control a lot of what is added to it. Paying bills on time, keeping debts manageable, and borrowing only for goals that are worth the cost are habits of responsibility, and they earn you a better price for money.

One more feature explains why more than one type exists. Inflation, which you studied in Module 3, quietly shrinks what a fixed payment can buy. If you lend $1,000 for ten years at a fixed rate, and prices rise a lot, you are repaid in dollars that buy less than the ones you lent. TIPS were designed to answer that worry. Their principal rises with the Consumer Price Index, so the value of your loan follows prices upward. A saver who cares about keeping buying power, not just dollars, has a reason to look at them.

💡 LOOK IT UP
Treasury yields change every day. You can see current figures on the Treasury Department's website. Look at a 2-year and a 10-year yield, and notice whether the longer one is higher or lower. Then ask yourself why. Economists have a name for the pattern that results, and you will find it when you look.

In the next lesson, you will meet the organization that influences the benchmark most directly: the Federal Reserve. You learned its tools in Module 3. Now you will see how it uses them to push on the price of money, and what that does to the rate on your own loan.
