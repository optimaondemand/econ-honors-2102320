How an Amortized Loan Works and What Sets Your Rate
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What is hiding inside a monthly payment of $761.78, and how would you know if a lender's offer was fair?
A loan can look simple: you borrow a sum, then pay a fixed amount every month. Underneath, each payment is two different things at once. This lesson opens the payment and shows you the arithmetic inside. Then you will read a real lender form, the way a careful borrower should, with every number checked.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to compute a fixed monthly payment with the standard formula, and show how the first payment splits into interest and principal.
2
I will be able to explain what sets the interest rate a lender offers: the benchmark, your credit, the term, and the down payment.
3

📚 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth a look before you read. Each one is bold and underlined in the text. Hover over it for a quick definition, or tap the speaker to hear it said.
Amortization /am-or-tih-ZAY-shun/
Paying off a loan gradually with regular, equal payments, so that each payment covers interest and also reduces the amount owed.
Annual Percentage Rate (APR) /AN-yoo-ul per-SEN-tij rayt/
The yearly cost of a loan, written as a percent, that includes the interest rate plus certain fees. It is not the same as the interest rate.
Escrow /ES-kroh/
An account where a lender holds part of your payment to pay your property taxes and home insurance for you.
Loan Estimate /lohn ES-tuh-mit/
A three-page form a lender must give you after you apply for a mortgage. It shows the loan's key terms and costs so you can compare offers.
Principal /PRIN-suh-pul/
The amount of money you borrowed and still owe, not counting interest.
Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI) /PRY-vit MOR-gij in-SHUR-uns/
Insurance that protects the lender, not you, if you stop paying. Lenders often require it when your down payment is small.
🧮 PART 1 · THE IDEA OF AMORTIZATION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A family borrows $162,000 and repays it in equal monthly payments over 30 years. About how much will they have paid back in total? (No wrong answer. You may change your mind.)

A loan is amortized when it is paid off gradually in equal payments. The word comes from an old term meaning "to kill off." Each month, part of your payment pays the interest that has built up, and the rest chips away at the principal, which is the amount you still owe. The payment stays the same, but the split between interest and principal changes every month.

Here is why the split changes. Interest is charged on what you still owe. At the beginning you owe a lot, so the interest is large. As you pay down the principal, you owe less, so the interest shrinks. The payment is fixed, so the part left over for principal grows. Early payments are mostly interest. Late payments are mostly principal. Keep that picture in mind, because the next lesson is built on it.

The formula

For a fixed-rate loan, the monthly payment comes from one formula. Here it is, with the symbols explained below it.

\[ M \;=\; P \cdot \frac{i}{1-(1+i)^{-n}} \]

In words: the payment equals the amount borrowed, times the monthly rate divided by one minus the discount factor. You do not need to be afraid of it. You only need to plug in three numbers.

SymbolMeaningIn our example
MThe monthly paymentWhat we are finding
PThe principal, the amount borrowed$162,000
iThe monthly interest rate: the yearly rate divided by 123.875% a year, so 0.03875 divided by 12
nThe number of monthly payments30 years times 12 months, which is 360
📝 PART 2 · A WORKED EXAMPLE, CHECKED

Our example is the sample loan on the government's model Loan Estimate, which you will read in Part 4. The borrower is buying a home for $180,000 and borrowing $162,000 for 30 years at a fixed rate of 3.875 percent. Let us compute the payment from scratch.

Step one is the monthly rate. Divide 0.03875 by 12 and you get \(i = 0.0032292\). Step two is the discount factor. Raise \(1 + i\) to the power of negative 360, which means one divided by \((1+i)\) multiplied by itself 360 times, and you get \((1+i)^{-360} = 0.313290\). Step three: one minus that number is 0.686710. Step four: divide the monthly rate by it, and you get 0.00470237. Step five: multiply by $162,000, and the result is $761.78 after rounding to the nearest cent.

That matches the monthly principal and interest printed on the lender's form, $761.78. When a calculation agrees with an independent source, you can trust it. If it did not, the right move is to find the mistake before going further.

Opening up the first payment

In month one, the interest is the full balance times the monthly rate: $162,000 times 0.0032292, which is $523.13 to the nearest cent. The other part of the payment, $761.78 minus $523.13, is $238.65. That is the only part that reduces what you owe. The new balance is $162,000 minus $238.65, which is $161,761.35.

YOUR FIRST $761.78 PAYMENT
Interest $523.13
Principal $238.65
More than two-thirds of the first payment is interest.
Try this in your notebook before you click: find the interest part of the second payment. Use the new balance after month one, and the same monthly rate. Then find the principal part.
The new balance is $161,761.35. Multiply by the monthly rate 0.0032292, and the interest is $522.35. The principal part is $761.78 minus $522.35, which is $239.43. Notice that the interest went down a little and the principal went up a little. That is the amortization at work, one month at a time.

Now the answer to the opening question. Over 360 months, the family will pay 360 times $761.78, which is $274,240.80. They borrowed $162,000, so the total interest is $112,240.80. That is about 69.3 percent of the amount borrowed. The total they repay is about 1.69 times what they received. Borrowing is not free, and a long loan is expensive in a way that is easy to miss when you only look at the monthly number.

🎯 PART 3 · WHAT SETS YOUR RATE

Where did 3.875 percent come from? Lenders build a rate in layers, as you saw in Lesson 8.01. The base is the benchmark, which follows Treasury yields and responds to the Fed's decisions from Lesson 8.02. On top of it, the lender adds a premium based on how risky you look and how risky the loan is.

FactorHow it can change your rateWho controls it
The benchmarkRises and falls with Treasury yields and the economyMarkets and the Fed, not you
Your credit historyA strong history of on-time payments lowers the premiumYou, over time
The term of the loanLonger loans carry more uncertainty and often a higher rateYou, when you choose the term
Your down paymentA bigger one lowers the lender's risk and can lower the rateYou, when you save
The type of loan and its featuresFixed or variable, and whether there are penaltiesYou and the lender

Your credit history shows up in a number. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes a credit score as "a prediction of your credit behavior," and says most scores run from 300 to 850. Scoring models look at things like your record of paying bills, how much debt you carry, how long your accounts have been open, how much of your available credit you are using, and recent applications for new credit. The Bureau also points out that you do not have just one score. It can vary by scoring model, by the kind of loan, and by the date.

One more choice shapes the rate. A fixed-rate loan keeps the same rate for its whole life, so your payment never changes because of the market. A variable-rate loan has a rate that moves with a benchmark, so the payment can rise or fall. Fixed rates are usually a little higher at the start in exchange for certainty. Variable rates can start lower but leave you exposed to the kind of rate increases you saw in Lesson 8.02. Neither is wrong. The question is which risk you are better able to carry.

The down payment matters in our example. The home costs $180,000 and the loan is $162,000, so the down payment is $18,000. That is 10 percent. Lenders usually see a loan as safer when the borrower has put in more of their own money. In the sample, the lender requires private mortgage insurance, which protects the lender, not the borrower, and which adds $82 a month for the first seven years. You will see it in the form below.

📜 PART 4 · READING THE LOAN ESTIMATE

Federal rules require a lender to give you a Loan Estimate within a few days of applying for a mortgage. It is three pages long, and its purpose is to let you compare offers from different lenders on the same layout. Below are the key lines from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's own sample, "H-24(B)," dated February 7, 2014. The names and the lender on it are made up. The numbers are exactly as printed.

Prepayment PenaltyYes. "As high as $3,240 if you pay off the loan during the first 2 years"A fee for paying the loan off early. The sample sets it at 2 percent of the balance for two years.

📜 PRIMARY SOURCE
"Annual Percentage Rate (APR) 4.274%. Your costs over the loan term expressed as a rate. This is not your interest rate."

"In 5 Years: $56,582. Total you will have paid in principal, interest, mortgage insurance, and loan costs. $15,773. Principal you will have paid off."
Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure, sample Loan Estimate H-24(B), Mortgage Loan Transaction Loan Estimate, Fixed Rate Loan, February 7, 2014 (page 3, "Comparisons").

Two items on the sample are easy to skip and worth understanding. The first is the escrow account. Rather than send your tax bill and insurance bill yourself, you pay a monthly amount, $206 in the sample, and the lender saves it up and pays the bills when they are due. That is convenient, and it protects the lender, since an unpaid tax bill can put a claim on the house. The second is closing costs, which are fees for things like the appraisal, the title search, and recording the sale. They are often forgotten when families plan a purchase, and they were $8,054 here, which is nearly 45 percent of the $18,000 down payment.

Study the two quotations. The APR, 4.274 percent, is higher than the 3.875 percent interest rate. The form tells you why in a single sentence: the APR expresses your costs over the loan term as a rate, and it is "not your interest rate." It folds in the loan's fees and other costs, so it is a fairer way to compare two lenders who charge different fees. If one lender offers a lower interest rate but charges high fees, the APR can show you which loan actually costs less.

Now the second quotation. After five years of payments, the family will have paid $56,582 in total, and only $15,773 of it will have gone to reduce the principal. That is about 28 percent. The rest went to interest, insurance, and loan costs. You can check the principal figure yourself with the amortization schedule: the principal paid in 60 months comes to about $15,773. The form and the formula agree.

How would you use the form to shop? Ask at least two lenders for a Loan Estimate on the same loan. Put the forms side by side. Compare the interest rate, the APR, the monthly payment, the cash to close, and any penalty for paying early. If one lender has a lower rate and a much higher APR, ask why. A careful borrower asks questions until the answers make sense, and does not sign anything while a number is still unexplained.

💡 A NOTE ABOUT ONE LINE WE DID NOT USE
The form's page 3 also lists a Total Interest Percentage of 69.45 percent. A straight calculation, total interest divided by the loan amount, gives about 69.3 percent. The two figures are close but not identical. The Loan Estimate has its own rules for counting, and we have not traced them here, so use this line only to compare loans on the same form. When two honest numbers disagree slightly, say so and keep working, rather than pretending they match.
