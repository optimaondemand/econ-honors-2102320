Course Overview

Economics Honors is an honors-level survey of the principles that govern how individuals, businesses, and nations make decisions under scarcity. Scholars examine the factors of production and economic systems, supply and demand and market structures, money and banking and the Federal Reserve, fiscal and monetary policy and the business cycle, and international trade. The course then puts those ideas to work on three questions of real weight: what happened to the American economy in the Great Depression, why command economies fail while free ones allow people to flourish, and what it costs an ordinary person to borrow money. The course builds toward a working understanding of the free enterprise system and its place in American civic life, and it satisfies Florida’s required-instruction component on the nature and importance of free enterprise to the United States economy.

What Scholars Will Learn to Do

●Explain scarcity, opportunity cost, and the factors of production as the foundation of every economic decision.●Analyze how supply, demand, and market structures interact to determine prices and shape competition.
●Evaluate the functions of money, the banking system, and the Federal Reserve’s role in a modern economy.●Apply principles of personal budgeting and credit to real financial decisions.
●Analyze the fiscal and monetary policy tools governments use to pursue economic stability.●Explain the business cycle and evaluate how policymakers respond to instability and externalities.
●Compare absolute and comparative advantage to explain the logic and benefits of international trade.●Evaluate trade barriers, free trade zones, and the circular flow of an open market economy.
●Explain the causes, course, and consequences of the Great Depression, and judge the policy responses with evidence rather than slogans.●State the theory of communism fairly, then use prices, knowledge, and incentives to explain why command economies fail.
●Calculate yields and loan payments, build an amortization schedule, and read a Loan Estimate.●Compare loan offers by their total cost and recommend one to a borrower in plain words.

On-Demand (Asynchronous) Learning

In the On-Demand section you move through the course module by module, at your own pace, guided by due dates that keep you on track. Because the course is On-Demand, there is no grade penalty for missing an individual due date, but any assignment not completed by the end of the quarter receives a zero, so steady pacing matters.

Attendance is measured by assignment completion. Per school policy, a scholar who completes no assignment for ten consecutive school days, without notifying the school of an absence in advance, may be dropped from the course. Completion means a sincere attempt: real effort on real work. Rushed work, wrong or corrupted files, irrelevant or nonsense submissions, AI-generated filler, and word-salad responses do not count.

You are never on your own. A live facilitator grades your work, gives feedback, and hosts a weekly study hall and VR workshops.

This course is also taught as a Live (synchronous) section that meets on a schedule on Optima’s VR campus. If that is the section you are enrolled in, use the Live syllabus instead of this one.

Using AI in This Course

Optima’s AI policy does not ask whether a scholar used AI. It asks whether AI was used honestly, skillfully, and in a way that genuinely built your own thinking. AI may support your work, but it may not do the reasoning an assignment asks of you.

Which AI tools you may use.

  • Every scholar. Any AI used for this course is school-controlled: tools built into Canvas assignments, set up to ask you questions and prompt your thinking rather than hand you answers. No assignment in this course requires a personal AI account.
  • Scholars under 14. Your facilitator may not ask, suggest, or require that you use an AI tool outside those school-controlled platforms. Your AI work for this course happens in Canvas.
  • Scholars 14 and older. Whether you use an outside AI tool for schoolwork is your family’s decision, not the school’s. It requires written parent or guardian permission on file with your facilitator, and your parent or guardian selects the tool, sets up the account, monitors it, and stays responsible for it. Optima does not provide, endorse, or manage personal AI accounts. Your facilitator’s part is to judge whether the AI was used well.

What is expected when you use AI.

  • Document it. Record the tool, the prompt you gave it, and the date. Using AI is not plagiarism. Turning in AI-generated work as entirely your own thought, with no documentation, is.
  • Verify it. AI states wrong things confidently, and you are responsible for every claim you submit, so check it against a credible source before it goes into your work. Verification is one of the skills this course grades.
  • Sound like yourself. Your work should read the way you write and speak in the rest of the course, take a real position rather than hedging, and be something you can explain on the spot when your facilitator asks a follow-up question you did not expect.
  • Keep the process, not just the product. Prompt logs, drafts, and revision notes are gradeable evidence of your thinking. When an assignment asks for them, they count toward your grade.

Some assignments limit or exclude AI so that you build the underlying skill yourself, and the assignment page will say so. The reason is practical: you cannot judge whether an AI’s answer is any good unless you can do the work without it.
