Course Overview

Economics Honors is an honors-level, one-semester survey of the principles that govern how individuals, businesses, and nations make decisions under scarcity. Scholars examine the factors of production and economic systems, supply and demand and market structures, money and banking and the Federal Reserve, fiscal and monetary policy and the business cycle, and international trade — building toward a working understanding of the free enterprise system and its place in American civic life. This course satisfies Florida’s required-instruction component on the nature and importance of free enterprise to the United States economy.

What Scholars Will Learn to Do

●Explain scarcity, opportunity cost, and the factors of production as the foundation of every economic decision. ●Analyze how supply, demand, and market structures interact to determine prices and shape competition.
●Evaluate the functions of money, the banking system, and the Federal Reserve’s role in a modern economy. ●Apply principles of personal budgeting and credit to real financial decisions.
●Analyze the fiscal and monetary policy tools governments use to pursue economic stability. ●Explain the business cycle and evaluate how policymakers respond to instability and externalities.
●Compare absolute and comparative advantage to explain the logic and benefits of international trade. ●Evaluate trade barriers, free trade zones, and the circular flow of an open market economy.

Live (Synchronous) Learning

In the Live section you join scheduled class meetings on Optima’s VR campus, where your teacher leads instruction in real time and you learn alongside classmates. The readings, quizzes, assignments, and projects are the same as the On-Demand course; what differs is the pacing — a Live section moves through the five modules together on the class schedule rather than at each scholar’s own pace, and class time is also used for discussion, questions, and guided practice.

Attendance is measured by presence and participation. You are expected to attend the scheduled live sessions, on time and ready to take part. If you must miss a class, notify the school in advance; your teacher will let you know how to make up the work and any material you missed. As in every Optima course, completing your work means a sincere attempt — real effort on real work — and assignments still open and close with each quarter.

You meet regularly with a teacher. The class gathers throughout the week in VR and on Microsoft Teams for live instruction and discussion.

This course is also offered as an On-Demand (asynchronous) section that scholars pace themselves. If that is the section you are enrolled in, use the On-Demand syllabus instead of this one.

Using AI in This Course

Optima’s AI policy does not ask whether a scholar used AI. It asks whether AI was used honestly, skillfully, and in a way that genuinely built your own thinking. AI may support your work, but it may not do the reasoning an assignment asks of you.

Which AI tools you may use.

  • Every scholar. Any AI used for this course is school-controlled: tools built into Canvas assignments, set up to ask you questions and prompt your thinking rather than hand you answers. No assignment in this course requires a personal AI account.
  • Scholars under 14. Your teacher may not ask, suggest, or require that you use an AI tool outside those school-controlled platforms. Your AI work for this course happens in Canvas.
  • Scholars 14 and older. Whether you use an outside AI tool for schoolwork is your family’s decision, not the school’s. It requires written parent or guardian permission on file with your teacher, and your parent or guardian selects the tool, sets up the account, monitors it, and stays responsible for it. Optima does not provide, endorse, or manage personal AI accounts. Your teacher’s part is to judge whether the AI was used well.

What is expected when you use AI.

  • Document it. Record the tool, the prompt you gave it, and the date. Using AI is not plagiarism. Turning in AI-generated work as entirely your own thought, with no documentation, is.
  • Verify it. AI states wrong things confidently, and you are responsible for every claim you submit, so check it against a credible source before it goes into your work. Verification is one of the skills this course grades.
  • Sound like yourself. Your work should read the way you write and speak in the rest of the course, take a real position rather than hedging, and be something you can explain on the spot, on camera or in VR, when your teacher asks a follow-up question you did not expect.
  • Keep the process, not just the product. Prompt logs, drafts, and revision notes are gradeable evidence of your thinking. When an assignment asks for them, they count toward your grade.

Some assignments limit or exclude AI so that you build the underlying skill yourself, and the assignment page will say so. The reason is practical: you cannot judge whether an AI’s answer is any good unless you can do the work without it.

Learning in Virtual Reality

Optima is a virtual-reality school. Alongside your Canvas coursework, you will join immersive sessions on Optima’s VR campus, where you meet teachers and fellow scholars, take part in live activities, and explore places and ideas you could not visit in an ordinary classroom. Keep your headset charged and ready for any scheduled VR sessions, and treat the VR campus with the same courtesy and seriousness you would a physical one. If you ever cannot get into VR for a session, tell your teacher — there is an alternative way to take part so you do not fall behind.

Being an Optima Scholar

Optima’s mission is an excellent, virtue-centered education, and virtue is at the center of this course as much as any other. Every scholar is expected to practice the seven virtues that shape life at Optima — courage, courtesy, honesty, perseverance, self-government, service, and responsibility. In practice that means: do your own honest work; treat everyone you meet in, through, or because of this course with respect, in writing, in discussion, and in VR; engage actively rather than passively; come to office hours to get help, meet other scholars, and get to know your teacher; and take responsibility for your own work and progress. You belong to a house and a community of scholars, not only to a course.
