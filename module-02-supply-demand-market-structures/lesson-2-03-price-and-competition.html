📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why doesn't Apple just lower its prices to beat every competitor?
You now know the four market structures. Today's question is what firms actually do inside those structures to win customers: compete on price, compete on everything except price, or — as most real firms do — blend both.

📚 PART 1 · TWO WAYS TO WIN A CUSTOMER
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When you choose one brand over another that does basically the same thing, what usually tips the decision? (No wrong answer.)

Price Competition

Price competition means winning customers mainly by charging less. It thrives where products are standardized and buyers are price-sensitive: grocery chains built on bulk purchasing and minimal frills, budget airlines that strip services to the essentials, e-commerce platforms using pricing algorithms to stay cheapest in real time. It democratizes access to goods, but it also compresses profit margins and can push firms toward under-investment in quality or workers if pursued too aggressively.

Nonprice Competition

Nonprice competition means winning customers through quality, design, service, or brand identity instead of a lower price — a tech company built on design and ecosystem rather than being the cheapest, a coffee chain curating an in-store experience rather than competing on the price of a cup. It builds loyalty that survives a price increase, and it drives innovation, but it requires sustained investment in brand and product, not just cost-cutting.

Most Firms Blend Both

In practice, price is often the "hook" that gets a customer's attention, and nonprice differentiation is what keeps them. Streaming services set an accessible subscription price, then compete on original content and recommendations. Retailers combine weekly discounts with a distinct store experience. Fast food chains run value menus while investing in loyalty apps and brand personality. Firms often shift the balance with conditions: price competition tends to matter more when consumers are cautious (a downturn); nonprice prestige and precedence tend to matter more when the economy is growing and customers have more to spend.

📚 KEY VOCABULARY

Market Share — the portion of total sales in a market controlled by a particular company or product.
Bulk Purchasing — buying large quantities of goods at once, often at a discount.
Frills — extra features or services added to a product beyond its basic function.
E-Commerce — the buying and selling of goods or services over the internet.
Algorithm — a step-by-step set of rules or calculations used to solve problems or make decisions.
Profit Margin — the percentage of revenue that remains as profit after expenses.
Permeate — to spread throughout or penetrate every part of something.
Habitually — done regularly or by established routine.
Lean — operating with minimal waste, focused on efficiency.
Democratize — to make something accessible to more people.
Discretionary — spending or actions that are optional rather than necessary.
Cultivate — to develop or nurture something over time.
Commodity — a basic good interchangeable with others of the same type.
Aspirational — inspiring people to strive for it because it represents higher status.
Intangible — something without physical form that still has value, like reputation.
Curate — to carefully select and organize items for presentation or appeal.
Exclusivity — limiting access to create a sense of uniqueness or privilege.
Witty — clever, humorous, quick-thinking.
Replicate — to copy or reproduce something, aiming for the same results.
Precedence — being considered more important or prioritized over something else.
Prestige — the respect or high status that comes from success, quality, or reputation.
