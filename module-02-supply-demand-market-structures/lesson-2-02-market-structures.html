📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Does it matter if a market has 4 sellers or 40,000?
Supply and demand set prices, but how many firms are doing the supplying changes everything about how that plays out. Today you'll compare four market structures, from the most competitive to the most concentrated.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to distinguish perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and monopoly.
2
I will be able to explain how entry barriers and product differentiation shape each structure.
3
I will be able to classify a real industry into one of the four structures and defend the classification.
📚 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth a look before you read. Each one is bold and underlined in the text. Hover over it for a quick definition, or tap the speaker to hear it said.
Collusion /kuh-LOO-zhun/
A secret or illegal agreement among competing firms to control prices or output.
Complement /KOM-plih-ment/
A good that is used together with another good.
Determinant of Demand /dih-TUR-mih-nunt uv dih-MAND/
A factor other than price, such as income or tastes, that shifts the whole demand curve.
Economies of Scale /ih-KON-uh-meez uv skayl/
Lower cost per unit that comes when a firm produces more.
Equilibrium /ee-kwuh-LIB-ree-um/
The price and quantity at which what buyers want to buy matches what sellers want to sell.
Natural Monopoly /NACH-er-ul muh-NOP-uh-lee/
A market where one firm can serve everyone at a lower cost than several competing firms could.
Patent /PAT-ent/
A government-granted right that gives an inventor the sole use of an invention for a limited time.
Price Ceiling /pryss SEE-ling/
A legal limit on how high a price can be.
Quantity Demanded /KWON-tih-tee dih-MAN-did/
How much of a good buyers are willing and able to buy at one particular price.
Substitute /SUB-stih-toot/
A good that can take the place of another good.
📚 PART 1 · FOUR MARKET STRUCTURES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of an industry with tons of small sellers (like farmers markets) versus one dominated by just a couple of huge companies. Which feels more familiar to you day to day? (No wrong answer.)

Perfect Competition (sometimes called pure competition)

The theoretical ideal: a huge number of small firms selling homogeneous, undifferentiated products, so buyers choose on price alone. No single firm can influence price — each is a "price taker." Free entry and exit push long-run economic profit toward zero, which keeps resources flowing to their most productive use. Agricultural commodities like wheat and corn come closest in the real world.

Monopolistic Competition

Many firms, but each differentiates its product through branding, quality, or ambiance — restaurants, clothing brands, hair salons. That differentiation gives firms some pricing power, but easy substitutes keep it limited. Entry and exit stay easy, so profits still trend toward zero long-run, but consumers get real variety in exchange for firms not producing at the lowest possible average cost.

Oligopoly

A small number of large, interdependent firms — automakers, airlines, smartphone makers. Each firm's pricing and output decisions directly affect its rivals, which produces strategies like price leadership and, illegally in most countries, collusion. High economies of scale and capital requirements create serious barriers to entry, which is what keeps the number of firms small in the first place.

    Monopoly

    A single firm supplies the entire market with no close substitutes, giving it real price-setting discretion. Monopolies arise from economies of scale (natural monopolies like water or electric utilities), government-granted patents, or exclusive control of a critical resource. They can fund heavy research and development with sustained profits, but typically produce less and charge more than a competitive market would. Governments respond with antitrust laws, price ceilings, or service requirements, especially for natural monopolies where breaking up the single provider would raise costs for everyone.

    📚 KEY VOCABULARY

    Firm — a business organization that produces goods or services to earn profits.
    Homogeneous — uniform in nature and indistinguishable from one another.
    Undifferentiated — lacking unique features, making a product identical to competitors' offerings.
    Ambiance — the overall atmosphere or mood of a physical space.
    Incremental — relating to small, gradual increases or improvements.
    Retention — a business's ability to keep its customers, employees, or resources over time.
    Prohibitive — costs or barriers so high that they discourage participation.
    Interdependent — a condition where entities rely on each other for outcomes.
    Collusion — a secret or illegal agreement between competing firms to control prices or output.
    Price Leadership — one dominant firm sets the price level and others follow.
    Supply Chains — the interconnected network of people and processes producing and delivering a product.
    Economies of Scale — cost advantages as production increases, lowering per-unit cost.
    Discretion — the freedom to make decisions based on one's own judgment.
    Patent — a government-granted exclusive right to an invention for a limited period.
    Marginal Cost / Marginal Revenue — the additional cost, or additional revenue, from producing/selling one more unit.
    Antitrust Laws — regulations preventing monopolistic practices and promoting fair competition.
    Predatory — aggressive or exploitative practices intended to weaken competitors.
    Entrench — to establish something so firmly it is difficult to change or remove.
    Natural Monopoly — a market where one firm can provide a good more cheaply than multiple competitors, due to economies of scale.
    Price Ceiling — a government-imposed limit on how high a price can be charged.
    Continuum — a continuous range or spectrum without clear divisions.
    Nuances — subtle differences or fine distinctions.
    Optimize — to make the most efficient use of resources to achieve a desired outcome.
    Stakeholder — any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, a business's actions.
    Foster — to encourage the growth or success of something.
