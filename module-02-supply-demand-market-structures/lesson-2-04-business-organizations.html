📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why would a founder ever give up sole control of their own business?
Every firm you studied last lesson had to pick a legal shape before it could compete on price or anything else. Today's four shapes — sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation, LLC — trade off control, liability, and access to capital in different ways.

📚 PART 1 · FOUR WAYS TO ORGANIZE A BUSINESS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
If you started a small business tomorrow, what would worry you more: losing full control of decisions, or being personally on the hook if something goes wrong? (No wrong answer.)

Sole Proprietorship

One owner, full autonomy, minimal paperwork to start — the simplest structure, common for coffee shops, freelancers, and small online sellers. Profits go straight to the owner and are taxed as personal income. The cost of that simplicity is unlimited personal liability: if the business incurs debt or gets sued, the owner's personal assets are exposed, and lenders often hesitate to extend credit without formal governance behind it.

Partnership

Two or more owners pool capital and expertise under a formal agreement. A general partnership splits management, profit, and liability equally among all partners; a limited partnership has at least one general partner who manages and bears full liability, plus limited partners who invest capital without day-to-day involvement or full liability exposure. Common in law and accounting firms, where pooling expertise across jurisdictions is valuable. Risk: shared liability can be amplified across multiple partners, and disagreements can strain or even force dissolution of the business.

Corporation

A separate legal entity from its owners. Shareholders get limited liability — their personal assets are protected — which is exactly what lets a corporation raise huge amounts of capital through issuance of stocks and bonds from thousands of investors at once. Corporations have perpetual existence and formal governance (boards, executives), but face heavy regulatory disclosure requirements and, in many cases, double taxation: the corporation pays tax on profits, then shareholders pay tax again on dividends.

⚡ COMMIT, THEN CHECK · WHY GIVE UP SOLE CONTROL?
A founder can run a business alone and keep full control. Why might the same founder choose to form a corporation and share control?
In your notebook, write one benefit and one cost of incorporating. Then click here to check your thinking.
Benefit: shareholders get limited liability, so their personal assets are protected. That is what lets a corporation raise large sums by selling stocks and bonds to thousands of investors at once. Costs: formal governance with boards and executives, heavy disclosure requirements, and often double taxation. The corporation pays tax on its profits, then shareholders pay tax again on dividends.

Limited Liability Company (LLC)

A hybrid: members get the limited liability of a corporation, but keep the operating flexibility of a partnership, including profit-sharing arrangements that do not have to match ownership percentages. LLCs are usually pass-through entities for tax purposes, avoiding the corporation's double taxation, while staying less formally encumbered than a corporation's board-and-shareholder structure. That combination has made the LLC a popular starting point for startups and service businesses.

📚 KEY VOCABULARY

Autonomy — the ability to make independent decisions without outside control.
Liability — a legal or financial obligation a person or business is responsible for.
Incur — to become subject to a cost, debt, or consequence as a result of an action.
Exposure — the level of risk or vulnerability to loss or outside influence.
Overhead Costs — ongoing expenses of running a business not tied to a specific product.
Infraction — a violation of a law, rule, or regulation.
Feasible — capable of being accomplished successfully.
General Partnership / Limited Partnership — structures where partners share management and liability equally, or where some partners invest with limited liability and no management role.
Infusion — the introduction of new resources, such as capital, into an organization.
Prevalent — widely existing or accepted.
Jurisdiction — the legal authority of a court or government to make decisions and enforce laws.
Dissolution — the formal closing or ending of a business or legal entity.
Shareholders — individuals or entities that own shares in a corporation.
Risk Diversification — spreading investments across different assets to reduce overall risk.
Issuance — creating and distributing financial securities such as stocks or bonds.
Stocks / Bonds — ownership shares in a company, or debt securities where investors lend money in exchange for interest.
Liquidity — the ease with which an asset can be converted into cash without losing value.
Equity — the value of ownership interest in a company.
Perpetual Existence — a corporation's ability to continue operating regardless of changes in ownership.
Continuity — the ongoing operation of an organization without major disruption.
Management Hierarchy — structured levels of authority and responsibility within an organization.
Disclosure — making relevant information public or available to stakeholders.
Dividends — payments made by a corporation to shareholders from profits.
Net Returns — profit from an investment after all costs, taxes, and expenses.
Mitigate — to reduce the severity, impact, or risk of something.
Ownership Percentages — the proportion of a business or asset an individual or group controls.
Intellectual Property — legally protected creations of the mind, such as inventions or trademarks.
Pass-Through Entity — a business structure where profits/losses are taxed on owners' personal returns, not at the business level.
Encumbered — restricted or burdened by debt, claims, or legal obligations.
Consultation / Consensus — seeking advice before deciding, or general agreement reached after discussion.
Stakes — the level of personal or financial interest someone has in an outcome.
Scrutiny — careful and detailed examination or inspection.
